(Received 17 August 2007; revised 15 February 2008)
The exciting phenomenon of the transit of Venus on June 8, 2004 was observed with great interest the world over. A recent transit after that was of Mercury on the night (in India) between 8th and 9th of November 2006.
The transits of Mercury and Venus occur when either of them is in conjunction with the Sun as observed from the Earth provided these are within the prescribed “ecliptic limits.” The transit of Venus is a less frequent phenomenon than that of Mercury. For example, after the transit of Venus in June 2004, the next occurrence will be in June 2012. After that, the subsequent Venus transit will be about 105.5 years later.
In Indian classical astronomy, the siddhāntic texts have discussed in detail the phenomenon of conjunctions of the Sun, the Moon and the planets, between any two of them as also with some important visible stars like *Rohiṇī* (Aldeberan), *Tiṣya* or *Puṣya* ($\delta$ Cancri), *Citrā* (Spica) etc.
In the present paper, we have discussed the actual working procedures, based on siddhāntas, of not only transits of Venus and Mercury but also of the occultations of planets and stars. The results are compared with those of modern astronomical procedures.

## 2. CONJUNCTIONS OF STARS AND PLANETS

Among the planets (*tārāgraha*), conjunction (*yuti*) takes place between any two of them and also with the Moon or the Sun. This phenomenon takes place when the longitudes of the two concerned bodies are equal and their latitudes are within permissible limits of difference even as in the case of the eclipses of the Sun and the Moon.
तारा ग्रहाणामन्योऽन्यं स्यातां युद्धसमागमौ ।
समागमः शशाङ्केन सूर्येणास्तमनं सह ॥
- *Sūrya Siddh.* Ch. 7. śl.1

If the conjunction is
(i) between two planets it is called *yuddha* (encounter)
(ii) between a planet and the Moon, then it is called *samāgama*. In modern parlance it is called ‘occultation’.
(iii) between a planet and the Sun, it is called *astamana* or *astaṅgata* (heliacal setting)
In particular if Mercury or Venus is in conjunction with the Sun, such an alignment is called ‘transit’. The recently witnessed transit of Venus on June 8th 2004 was truly spectacular.

## 3. CONJUNCTION - ELAPSED OR TO OCCUR

तयोः प्रयायिनोरेवं वक्रिणोस्तु विपर्ययात् ॥

If the longitude of the faster moving body is greater than that of the slower one, the conjunction (*samyoga*) is passed. Otherwise the conjunction is yet to happen. This is the case when the two bodies are moving eastward (direct motion). However if both the bodies are retrograde (*vakrī*) the reverse order is followed.

## 4. THE INSTANT OF CONJUNCTION OF TWO BODIES

The famous Indian astronomer of the early 16th century, Gaṇeśa Daivajña (epoch: March 19, 1520) describes the method of determining the instant of conjunction and also whether it is past or yet to occur in his *Grahalāghava* (GL):
ऋजुगतिखगयोस्तु वक्रयोर्वा विवरकला गतिजान्तरेण भक्तः ।
गतिजयुतिहृता यदैकवक्री युतिगता प्रगताप्तवासरैः स्यात् ॥
- *Grahalāghavam*, Ch. 13, śl. 3\*

This *śloka* explains how to obtain days (and *ghaṭīs* etc) of *gata* or *gamya* instant of conjunction.
(i) If both the bodies are in direct motion or in retrograde motion then divide the difference between their positions by the difference between their daily motions, both the values expressed in the same unit of angle viz., *kalā* (minute) or *aṃśa* (degree). The result will be in days, *ghaṭīs* etc. This gives the time in days etc of the *gata* (elapsed) or *gamya* (to be covered).
(ii) Of the two bodies if one is in direct motion and the other in retrograde motion then divide the difference between their positions by the sum of their daily motions.
**Example:** On May 2, 1610(G), Sunday (*Cakras* = 8, *Ahargaṇa* = 778) at the sunrise the true longitude of Kuja, $P_1 = 10^R \ 6^\circ \ 35' \ 9''$ and the true longitude of Śani, $P_2 = 10^R \ 2^\circ \ 58' \ 44''$ and both are in direct motion. The number of days for *yuti* is given by

$$\frac{P_1 - P_2}{DP_1 - DP_2}$$

the difference between the longitudes of Kuja and Śani:

$$P_1 - P_2 = 3^\circ 36' 25'' = 216' 25''$$

and the difference between their daily motions

$$DP_1 - DP_2 = 42' 50'' - 3' 3'' = 39' 47''.$$

$$\therefore \text{Days for } yuti = \frac{216' 25''}{39' 47''} = 5^d 26^h 23^{vig}$$

Since the longitude of Kuja is greater than that of Śani, the *yuti* is over by $5^d 26^h 23^{vig}$ before the given date.

## 8. BHEDA AND ABHEDA CONJUNCTIONS

Even as in the cases of solar and lunar eclipses, two bodies may have the same longitude but those may not form technically a “*bhedayuti*” in the sense of one body eclipsing the other.
At the instant of conjunction if the *mānaikyakhaṇḍa* i.e. sum of the semi-diameters of the planets, is less than the algebraic difference of their latitudes there is no “*bheda yoga*.” What is of interest for further computations and observations is the *bheda yoga* – one planet “piercing” the other. This happens when the sum of the semi-diameters of the bodies is greater than the algebraic difference of their latitudes.
In case there is *bheda yoga*, the planet which is “lower” is treated as Moon and the “higher” one as the Sun. In that case the effects of parallax on the longitude and latitude namely *lambana* and *nati* are determined as in the case of a solar eclipse.
**Note:** Between two conjuncting bodies the *higher* and the *lower* between them are decided as follows:
(i) If both bodies have northern latitudes then the one with the higher latitude is considered as *higher*.
(ii) If both the bodies have southern latitudes then the one with numerically lesser latitude is considered as the *higher* body.
(iii) If the two bodies have their latitudes in opposite directions then the one with the northern latitude is the *higher* body.
In general, the body towards the north is considered as the *higher* body.
**Remark:** While the conjunctions in longitudes of any two planets occur frequently, in the special sense of one “eclipsing” the other, such occurrences are extremely rare. This is so because the angular diameters of the planets are very small and therefore the sum of their semi-diameters is rarely greater than the difference between their latitudes.
However, the commentator of *Karaṇakutūhalam*, Sumatiharṣa works out in detail the conjunction of Guru and Śukra which took place on *Śālivāhana śaka* 1541, *Vaiśākha kṛṣṇa* 14, Sunday. Though it was a case of a perfect conjunction (*bheda yoga*) it was not visible at the given place since the planets were below the horizon.

## 9. TRANSITS OF VENUS AND MERCURY

We provide below a procedure, evolved by us based on our traditional *siddhāntas* and inspired by the collected papers of the late Prof. T. S. Kuppanna Sastry.⁷ This algorithm is specially designed for transits of Śukra and Budha. This procedure is different from the one adopted in modern astronomy.
It is important to note that for a transit the concerned inferior planet is always retrograde (*vakra*) and in *inferior* conjunction.
(i) The true instant of conjunction in longitudes of the true Sun and the true planet is determined.
**Note:** In what follows, the parameters are represented by convenient-to-remember multi-letter notations like *SDIA*, *PAR*, *HDUR* etc. for the advantage of computer programming.
(ii) At the instant of true conjunction, the following parameters are computed:
(1) True positions of the Sun and the planet
(2) True daily rates of motion of the two bodies (*SDM* and *PDM*)
(3) The geocentric latitude of the planet $\beta$ (BETA)
(4) The horizontal parallaxes of the two bodies ($PAR_1$ and $PAR_2$)
(5) Rate of change of planet’s latitude per $nāḍī$, $P$
(6) Angular diameters of the bodies (*SDIA* and *PDIA*)
[Note: Items (2) to (6) are in minutes of arc (*kalās*)]
(7) The rate of motion of (planet $-$ Sun) per $nāḍī$:
$vyarkagraha\ nāḍīgati = (PDM - SDM)/60$
(8) Let the combined parallax, $PAR = PAR_1 - PAR_2$
(9) The sum of the combined parallax and the semi-diameters

$$D = PAR + \frac{SDIA + PDIA}{2}$$

---

(10) The sum of the parallax with the difference of the semi-diameters

$$D' = PAR + \frac{SDIA - PDIA}{2}$$

(11) The conditions for the occurrence of a transit and its totality:
(i) If $|\beta| < D$, the *transit occurs*.
(ii) If $|\beta| < D'$, the *transit is total*.
(12) The middle (MIDDLE) of the transit is obtained by applying a small correction to the instant of conjunction. This correction is given by

$$\frac{-pP}{\dot{m}^2 + P^2}\ nāḍī \text{ or approximately } \frac{99}{100} \frac{|\beta|}{\dot{m}}\ nāḍī$$

where $p = \beta$ and $P = d\beta$, the rate of change in planet’s latitude per $nāḍī$ (see **Appendix**)
(13) Half duration of the transit is given by

$$HDUR = \frac{\sqrt{D^2 - \beta^2}}{\dot{m}}\ nāḍī$$

(14) Half duration of the totality is given by

$$THDUR = \frac{\sqrt{(D')^2 - \beta^2}}{\dot{m}}\ nāḍīs$$

**Summary of the transit:**
(i) Beginning of transit (External Ingress) : $BEGG = MIDDLE - HDUR$
(ii) Beginning of totality (Internal Ingress) : $BEGGT = MIDDLE - THDUR$
(iii) Middle of the transit (greatest obscuration) : $MIDDLE$
(iv) End of totality (Internal Egress) : $ENDT = MIDDLE + THDUR$
(v) End of the transit (External Egress) : $TREND = MIDDLE + HDUR$

## 10. SUPERIOR CONJUNCTION OF AN INFERIOR PLANET WITH THE SUN

The transits of the inferior planets, Venus and Mercury discussed in the previous section occur when these planets have an *inferior conjunction* with the Sun. Of course these are subject to their respective transit limits.
An interesting situation, rarely discussed, is when an *inferior* planet has a *superior* conjunction with the Sun. In the case of a transit the inferior planet comes in between the earth and the Sun. This is the case of an inferior conjunction of the planet. On the other hand, in the case of a superior conjunction the Sun hides the inferior planet i.e. the Sun lies between the earth and the planet.
In Indian classical astronomical texts, the two situations are not explicitly discussed. However, their circumstances can be worked out by the general classical method of planetary conjunctions (*grahayuti*) discussed in this paper earlier.
In fact, the cases of inferior and superior conjunctions of an inferior planet can be easily determined from the Indian siddhāntic procedure.
In the case of an inferior conjunction the *śīghrakendra* of the planet is $180^{\circ}$ whereas for the superior conjunction the *śīghrakendra* vanishes. More interestingly, for an inferior conjunction the *śīghrakarṇa* of the planet is the difference between the heliocentric distances of the earth and the planet. On the other hand, for a superior conjunction the *śīghrakarṇa* is the sum of the heliocentric distances. In fact for an inferior planet the *śīghrakarṇa* is a measure of its geocentric distance. ## 14. ROHIṆĪ ŚAKAṬA BHEDA

The *Rohiṇī śakaṭa bheda* (RSB) is an interesting configuration in which an eligible heavenly body enters the region marked by a group of stars including and very close to the *yogatāra* (junction star) Rohiṇī (Aldebaran). In Indian astronomical literature we find frequent references to RSB. The phrase *śakaṭa bheda* means “piercing the cart” (of Rohiṇī).
The eligible stake-holders of the RSB phenomenon are the Moon, Mars and Saturn. Among the traditional texts, it is the *Grahalāghavam* (GL)

## 15. RATIONALE FOR MOON’S RSB

The *Rohiṇī* asterism, according to Indian traditional texts, comprises five visible stars with southern latitudes in the constellation of *Vṛṣabha* (Taurus). The celestial (sidereal) longitudes of the stars in the above group range from $45^\circ 46'$ to $49^\circ 45'$ of which the central longitude is about $47^\circ$. The latitudes of these stars vary from $2^\circ 36'$ to $5^\circ 47'$ to the south of the ecliptic considering the measure of one *aṅgula* as 3 arc-minutes, the above range comes to 52 to 116 *aṅgulas*. Therefore for a body to enter the cart of Rohiṇī it must have its southern latitude more than about $2^\circ .5$ i.e. 50 *aṅgulas*.

We have the traditional expression for the southern latitude (*śara*) of the Moon :

$$\beta = -270' \sin(M - R) = 270' \sin(R - M)$$

This gives the range for Moon’s node Rāhu from about $80^\circ .4$ to $193^\circ .6$ i.e. from the beginning of *Punarvasu* to the middle of *Svātī*. However from the modern expression for the Moon’s latitude the leading term is $18461'' \sin F$ where $F = M - R$, the mean distance of the Moon from its ascending node. Taking the sidereal longitude of the Moon $M = 47^\circ$, the range for sidereal Rāhu is from $77^\circ .47$ to $196^\circ .53$.
The Moon’s sidereal period is about 27.32166 days. Rāhu’s sidereal period being 6793.47 days, it takes about 2246.74 days to cover the above prescribed range which is of length $119^\circ .06$. During this period of Rāhu’s stay in that range, the sidereal Moon comes to $47^\circ$ about 82 times. This means that in the course of Rāhu’s sidereal period of about 18.6 years, the maximum number of times the Moon enters the *Rohiṇī* cart is 82 times successively.
Now, out of Rāhu’s sidereal period of 6793.47 days (18.6 years) the remaining part during which the Moon **does not** hit the *Rohiṇī śakaṭa* is about 12.45 years. However, the RSB by the Moon with Rāhu at a particular position, in the prescribed range repeats once in about 55.8 years.

Guru’s semi-diameter $= 16".26936 \equiv d_2$
$D = (PAR_1 - PAR_2) + (d_1 + d_2) = 34".074154 = 0'.5679025$
$p = \beta_1 - \beta_2 = +16'$
Since $D < p$, the conjunction is **not** a *bheda yoga* (“close conjunction” in the sense of an eclipse).

## 18. CONJUNCTION OF GURU WITH TIṢYA NAKṢATRA

In the *Ṛgveda saṃhitā* (*ṚV*), hailed as the most ancient literary work, and also in the *Atharvaveda* (*AV*), Jupiter’s conjunction with the *Tiṣya* (*Puṣya* i.e. $\delta$ Cancri) *nakṣatra* is mentioned in the following *mantra*:
बृहस्पतिः प्रथमं जायमानो महो ज्योतिषः परमे व्योमन् ।
सप्तस्यस्तु विजातो रवेण सप्तरश्मिर्धमत् तमांसि ॥
- *ṚV*, 4.50, 4; *AV*, 1088.4
“Bṛhaspati, when being born in the highest heaven of supreme light, seven-mouthed multi-form (combined) with sound and seven-rayed, has subdued the darkness.”
There is a more specific reference to the location of Jupiter in the following *mantra* from the *Taittirīya saṃhitā*:
बृहस्पतिः प्रथमं जायमानस्तिष्यं नक्षत्रमभिसम्बभूव
- *Tai. Saṃ.* 3.1.5
“*Bṛhaspati*, when first appearing, rose in front of the *Tiṣya* (*Puṣya*) asterism.”
Again, the conjunction of Guru with *Puṣya nakṣatra* ($\delta$ Cancri) being a rare event, it is worth investigating when this special conjunction took

**Example: Conjunction of Guru and Śukra on January 3/4, 1818 A.D.**
The instant of conjunction (in longitude) $T_0$ of Guru and Śukra: $3^h 21^m.53$ a.m. (IST). At the instant of conjunction, we have
Sāyana Guru = Sāyana Śukra = 266°34'48"
Śukra’s latitude, $\beta_1 = 17'.54709$ and Guru’s latitude, $\beta_2 = 17'.24695$

Parallax of Śukra, $PAR_1 = 5".452357$
Parallax of Guru, $PAR_2 = 1".4164$

$$D = PAR + d_1 + d_2 = 24".034563$$

The instants of beginning (*sparśa*) and the end (*mokṣa*), as in an eclipse, are given by

$$T_0 - \frac{pP}{\dot{m}^2 + P^2} \mp \frac{\sqrt{D^2 - p^2}}{\dot{m}} \dots(1)$$

where (i) $P = (D\beta_1 - D\beta_2)$, the difference in the daily rates of change in latitudes of the two bodies; and
(ii) $\dot{m} = VDM - JDM$. Here $VDM$ and $JDM$ are respectively the true daily motions of Venus and Jupiter.
In the example considered, we have

$$D\beta_1 = -5'.05634, \quad D\beta_2 = -0'.15717, \quad VDM = 2^\circ30'34", \quad JDM = 0^\circ26'47"$$

$$\therefore P = -5'.05634 + (0'.15717) = -4'.89917$$

$$\dot{m} = 2^\circ30'34" - 0^\circ26'47" = 2^\circ3'47" = 123'.78333$$

In expression (1), if $T_0$ is taken as the instant of conjunction, then the second term $\frac{-pP}{\dot{m}^2 + P^2}$ is the time interval from the instant of conjunction to the "middle" of conjunction i.e. of maximum obscuration. This term can be called "correction" to get the middle.
The third term in (1) gives the *half-duration*. When this value is subtracted from and added to the "middle" we get the beginning and the end of the conjunction respectively.
In the current example, from (1) we have "correction" $= +8.28$ sec. and half-duration $= 3^m5^s.28$ Therefore, the instant of beginning ($sparśa$) is $3^h18^m34^s.8$ (IST). The end of the conjunction ($mokṣa$) is $3^h24^m45^s.36$ (IST) on January 4, 1818 A.D. The middle is $3^h21^m40^s.08$.
**Remark:** It is important to note that conjunctions of planets take place quite often in the sense of their longitudes being equal. But the difference in their latitudes most often exceeds the sum of their semi-diameters which are very small as compared to those of the Sun and the Moon. However once in a way, though rarely as in the case of the example considered above, a "close" conjunction as in the case of an eclipse can take place. Such a phenomenon is called $bheda\ yoga$, i.e. "piercing" conjunction.

## 19. CONCLUSION

We have discussed at length in the preceding sections, conjunctions, transits and occultations between the heavenly bodies, by the Indian classical astronomical procedures. For this purpose we have explained the $siddhāntic$ algorithms for computing the important parameters like the angular diameters ($bimba$), the nodes ($pātas$), the latitudes ($śaras$), distinction between the $bheda$ and $abheda$ conjunctions and then finally the circumstances of the phenomena of transit and occultation.
We have observed that the Indian classical procedures, with necessary but minimal modifications, yield results which are comparable to those obtained by modern procedures.

### Latitudes, Inclinations and Nodes of Planets

In section 7, the determination of the celestial latitude ($śara$) according to the Indian classical texts has been presented and an example is also worked out. Essentially, the *geocentric* latitude is given by

$$Śara = \frac{\text{Maximum } śara \times Bhujajy\bar{a}}{Ś\bar{i}ghrakar\d{n}a} kal\bar{a}s$$

(cancelling the $trijy\bar{a}\ R$ both in the numerator and the denominator).
It is interesting to note that among Indian astronomers the Kerala astronomer, Nīlakaṇṭha Somayāji (1500 A.D.) was the first to explain clearly the *geocentric* latitude of a planet (see M.S.Sriram[^12]). In his cited paper M.S.Sriram has clearly explained the relation between the heliocentric and the geocentric latitudes of a planet considering the Keplerian model.

If $\beta_S$ and $\beta_E$ are respectively the heliocentric and the geocentric latitudes of a planet and $i_h$ is the inclination of the planet’s orbit with the ecliptic then $\beta_S \approx i_h \sin(\lambda_P - \lambda_N)$ where $\lambda_P$ and $\lambda_N$ are respectively the true heliocentric longitudes of the planet and the node. ### Maximum obscuration and half-duration of the phenomenon

Suppose the conjunction (in longitude) of two heavenly bodies occurs at time $T_0$. Let $p$ be the difference in their latitudes and $P$ the hourly rate of change in the difference in their celestial latitudes. If $\dot{m}$ is the hourly rate of change in the difference of the two celestial bodies, then after $t$ hours from the instant of their conjunction $T_0$, the distance between the centres of the two bodies is

$$\sqrt{(\dot{m}t)^2 + (p + Pt)^2}$$

Now, the phenomenon (eclipse, transit or occultation) commences ($sparśa$) and ends ($mokṣa$) when the rims of the two bodies just touch each other. At these two instants the distance between their centres equals the sum of their "apparent" semi-diameters. Here, the word "apparent" means the appearance of the discs affected by *parallax*.
Thus, at the commencement of the phenomenon, we have

$$(\dot{m}t)^2 + (p + Pt)^2 = d^2 \qquad \dots(1)$$

[^12]: M.S. Sriram, *Planetary Models in Indian and Greek Astronomical Traditions*, 500 Years of Tantrasangraha, IIAS, Shimla, 2002.

550                     INDIAN JOURNAL OF HISTORY OF SCIENCE
where $d$ is the sum of the "apparent" semi-diameters of the two bodies, that is

$$d = PAR + \frac{1}{2}(SDIA + PDIA) \qquad \dots(2)$$

$$\text{or} \qquad d = PAR + \frac{1}{2}(MDIA + PDIA) \qquad \dots(3)$$

Here, (2) and (3) apply respectively for the *transit* (of Venus or Mercury across the Sun) and the *occultation* (by the Moon). The angular diameters (*bimba*) of the Sun, the Moon and the planet are denoted respectively by $SDIA$, $MDIA$ and $PDIA$. The difference in parallaxes of the two bodies is denoted by $PAR$.
The time $t$ in hours (from $T_0$) at which the phenomenon commences or ends is given by the solution of the quadratic equation (1). Solving (1), we get

$$t = -\frac{pP}{\dot{m}^2 + P^2} \mp \sqrt{\frac{p^2 P^2}{(\dot{m}^2 + P^2)^2} + \frac{(p^2 - d^2)}{(\dot{m}^2 + P^2)}} \qquad \dots(4)$$

In the expression (4), the second term (with square-root) gives the half-duration of the phenomenon and the first term is the "correction" to the instant of conjunction (longitude) to get the instant of maximum obscuration. The negative and positive signs in (4) are used respectively for the *beginning* and the *end* of the phenomenon. It is important to note that in (4), all the quantities have the same angular unit (say arc-minutes) and the rates of change $\dot{m}$ and $P$ are per hour.
