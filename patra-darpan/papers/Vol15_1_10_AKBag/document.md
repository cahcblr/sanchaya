# INDIAN LITERATURE ON MATHEMATICS DURING 1400—1800 A.D.

A. K. BAG
Indian National Science Academy
New Delhi 110002
(Received 30 August 1978)
> The available Indian literature on mathematics during 1400-1800 A.D. can be broadly classified into two main categories. In the first category belong the Sanskrit works, or the works written in scripts of the regional Indian languages. Though the scripts are different, the language of these scripts is mainly Sanskrit and bears Indian tradition both in content and character. The major portion of these works are commentaries on the works of $S\bar{u}rya$ $Siddh\bar{a}nta$, $\bar{A}ryabha\d{t}iya$, $L\bar{i}l\bar{a}vat\bar{i}$, $B\bar{i}jaga\d{n}ita$, $Siddh\bar{a}nta$ $Siroma\d{n}i$, and some other well-known works of the ancient period, and contribute little to the knowledge of mathematics. The commentators of this period were perhaps content with the preservation and transmission of knowledge from one generation to the other. Their studies gave some impetus to the studies of the scholars like $M\bar{a}dhava$, $Parame\acute{s}vara$, $N\bar{i}laka\d{n}\d{t}ha$, $\acute{S}a\dot{n}kara$, $Jye\d{s}\d{t}hadeva$, $Acyuta$ $Pi\d{s}\bar{a}ra\d{t}i$, etc., for about 500 years starting from fourteenth century A.D. These scholars made some break-through by introducing the idea of series in calculating the value of $pi$, sine-table, discovery of the sine and cosine series and made many other innovations. The second category of mathematical literature constitute the Persian and Arabic works developed mostly under the patronage of the Mughal rulers. These were mainly written for readers of Persian who knew no other language and had no access in standard Sanskrit, Persian and Arabic mathematical works. Many standard mathematical works were brought here from outside India. Some scholars tried to translate or write commentaries on the available works without trying to correlate with the available Indian knowledge. A few scholars like $Mun\bar{i}\acute{s}vara$, $Kamal\bar{a}kara$, $Jagann\bar{a}th$ $Pa\d{n}\d{d}it$ tried to make a synthesis of the available knowledge but their contribution appears to be negligibly small. The real contribution lies in the effort of Raja Jai Singh who used the observatories at Delhi, Jaipur, Mathura, Banaras and Ujjain to prepare accurate astronomical tables. In the paper, an attempt has been made to make analysis of these two categories of mathematical literature in the period.

## 1. WORKS IN SANSKRIT LITERATURE

Thousands of works were written in the period. A few of the prominent works by well-known scholars have been summerized to have an idea of the type and trend of mathematical literature.[^1]
$Ga\dot{n}g\bar{a}dhara$ ($c.$ 1400), the son of $Govardhana$ and grandson of $Div\bar{a}kara$, was an inhabitant of $Jambus\bar{a}g\bar{a}ranagar$. His commentary $Ga\d{n}it\bar{a}m\d{r}tas\bar{a}gar\bar{i}$ is a commentary on the $L\bar{i}l\bar{a}vat\bar{i}$ of $Bh\bar{a}skar\bar{a}c\bar{a}rya$. It appears almost verbatim of the original work.
$M\bar{a}dhava$ ($c.$ 1400) of $Sa\dot{n}gamagr\bar{a}ma$ near Cochin was a well-known scholar in astronomy and mathematics.[^2] He is referred to as $golavid$ by his student $Parame\acute{s}vara$ and $N\bar{i}laka\d{n}\d{t}ha$. His $Ve\d{n}v\bar{a}roha$ gives a method for the computation of the moon and $Sphu\d{t}acandr\bar{a}pti$ accounts for calculation of true moon. He is also known to have written $Lagnaprakara\d{n}a$ and $Mah\bar{a}jy\bar{a}n\bar{a}yana$ $prakara\d{n}a$. Apart from these works, a number of these verses of $M\bar{a}dhava$ are quoted by later astronomers like $N\bar{i}laka\d{n}\d{t}ha$, $Somay\bar{a}ji$, $N\bar{a}r\bar{a}ya\d{n}a$ (commentator of the $L\bar{i}l\bar{a}vat\bar{i}$), $\acute{S}a\dot{n}kara$ (commentator of $Tantra$ $Sa\dot{m}graha$), etc.
$Parame\acute{s}vara$ ($c.$ 1430), a resident of South Malabar in Kerala, was the student of $Rudra$, $N\bar{a}r\bar{a}ya\d{n}a$ and $M\bar{a}dhava$. He is well-known for his lucid commentaries and knowledge in mathematics and astronomy. Among his works, the $Bha\d{t}ad\bar{i}pik\bar{a}$ (commentary on $\bar{A}ryabha\d{t}iya$ of $\bar{A}ryabha\d{t}a$-I), $Karmad\bar{i}pik\bar{a}$ (comm. on the $Mah\bar{a}bh\bar{a}skar\bar{i}ya$ of $Bh\bar{a}skara$ I), $Parame\acute{s}var\bar{i}$ (commentary on the $Laghubh\bar{a}skar\bar{i}ya$ of $Bh\bar{a}skara$-I), $Siddh\bar{a}ntad\bar{i}pik\bar{a}$ (comm. on the $Mah\bar{a}bh\bar{a}skar\bar{i}yabh\bar{a}\d{s}ya$ of $Govindasv\bar{a}mi$), $Vivara\d{n}a$ (commentary on the $S\bar{u}ryasiddh\bar{a}nta$ and $L\bar{i}l\bar{a}vat\bar{i}$), $D\d{r}gga\d{n}ita$ ($d\d{r}k$ system), $Golad\bar{i}pik\bar{a}$ (spherical geometry and astronomy), $Graha\d{n}ama\d{n}\d{d}a\d{n}a$ (computation of eclipses), $Graha\d{n}avy\bar{a}khy\bar{a}d\bar{i}pik\bar{a}$ (on the rational of the theory of eclipses), $V\bar{a}kyakara\d{n}a$ (on the methods of the derivation of the several astronomical tables) besides others are well-known. $Parame\acute{s}vara$ was a practical astronomer and realised the limitations of previous scholars in the calculation of eclipses and devised correction for finding mean position of the sun, moon, apsis, node, etc.

He is the author of $\bar{A}ryabha\d{t}iyabh\bar{a}\d{s}ya$, $Tantra$ $Sa\dot{m}graha$ (tantra collection), $Graha\d{n}anir\d{n}aya$ (computation of lunar and solar eclipses), $Golas\bar{a}ra$ (quintessence of spherical astro-

$Yallaya$ (1482), son of $\acute{S}r\bar{i}dhar\bar{a}c\bar{a}rya$, was the pupil of $S\bar{u}rya$.[^3] He is well-known for his works $\bar{A}ryabha\d{t}\bar{i}ya$, $Vy\bar{a}khy\bar{a}na$, (commentary on the $\bar{A}ryabha\d{t}\bar{i}ya$ of $\bar{A}ryabha\d{t}a$), $Jyoti\d{s}adarpa\d{n}a$, $Kalpavalli$ (commentary on the $S\bar{u}ryasiddh\bar{a}nta$), $Laghum\bar{a}nasa$ $Kalpalat\bar{a}$ (a commentary on the $Laghum\bar{a}nasa$ of $Ma\tilde{n}jul\bar{a}c\bar{a}rya$), and $Ga\d{n}itasa\dot{m}graha$ (a treatise on arithmetic).
$\acute{S}a\dot{n}kara$ $V\bar{a}riar$ ($c.$ 1500-1560), the brother and student of $N\bar{i}laka\d{n}\d{t}ha$ is the author of $Laghuviv\d{r}ti$, a scholarly commentary on the $Tantrasa\dot{m}graha$. $\acute{S}a\dot{n}kara$ seems to have well-versed in astronomy and in following his brother $N\bar{i}laka\d{n}\d{t}ha$. It is expressed by $N\bar{i}laka\d{n}\d{t}ha$ in some of the passages that he had complete faith in his brother in studies of mathematics and astronomy. He wrote another work, $Kriy\bar{a}kramakar\bar{i}$, an elaborate commentary on the $L\bar{i}l\bar{a}vat\bar{i}$ of $Bh\bar{a}skara$ II giving rational and proof of the theorems and formulae. The work is more or less identical with the $Karmaprad\bar{i}pak\bar{a}$ of $N\bar{a}r\bar{a}y\d{n}a$, only difference is that $\acute{S}a\dot{n}kara$'s work is more elaborate.
$Jye\d{s}\d{t}hadeva$ ($c.$ 1500—1600) is a well-known Kerala scholar on mathematics and astronomy. His $Yuktibh\bar{a}\d{s}\bar{a}$ (rational explanation) was written to provide the basic equipment needed for the study of computation of planetary movemets as depicted in the $Tantrasa\dot{m}graha$. The work is divided into two parts, the first comprising of arithmetic, geometry and algebra and the second exclusively devoted to the astronomy, spherical trigonometry and allied subjects. It gives the rational or derivation of all theorems and formulae then in use among the astronomers.
$Cakradhara$ ($c.$ 1500), son of $Varu\d{n}a$, wrote his $Yantracint\bar{a}ma\d{n}i$, a treatise on astronomical instruments in four chapters, viz. $Yantraprakara\d{n}as\bar{a}dhana$, $Tripra\acute{s}n\bar{a}dhik\bar{a}ra$, $Grah\bar{a}nayan\bar{a}dhik\bar{a}ra$ and $Prak\bar{i}r\d{n}\bar{a}dhy\bar{a}ya$. He also produced a commentary on his own $Yantracint\bar{a}ma\d{n}i$.
$J\bar{n}\bar{a}nar\bar{a}ja$ ($c.$ 1503) was the son of $N\bar{a}gan\bar{a}tha$ of $P\bar{a}rthapura$. He is well-known for his $Siddh\bar{a}nta$-$sundara$ dealing with astronomy. The $Sphu\d{t}\bar{a}dhy\bar{a}ya$ chapter contains sine table. His $Sundarasiddh\bar{a}ntab\bar{i}ja$ is another work on algebra which was written as a sequel to $Bh\bar{a}skara$ II's $B\bar{i}jaga\d{n}ita$.

$S\bar{u}ryad\bar{a}sa$ ($c.$ 1541), son of $J\bar{n}\bar{a}nar\bar{a}ja$, wrote two commentaries, viz. $Ga\d{n}it\bar{a}m\d{r}tak\bar{u}pik\bar{a}$, a commentary on the $L\bar{i}l\bar{a}vat\bar{i}$ of $Bh\bar{a}skara$ II, and $S\bar{u}ryaprak\bar{a}\acute{s}a$-$B\bar{i}javy\bar{a}khy\bar{a}$, a commentary on the $B\bar{i}jaga\d{n}ita$ of $Bh\bar{a}skara$ II.
$Ra\dot{n}gan\bar{a}tha$ ($c.$ 1578) son of $Ball\bar{a}la$ and father of $Mun\bar{i}\acute{s}vara$ wrote a commentary on the $S\bar{u}ryasiddh\bar{a}nta$. The commentary is well-known for its style and lucidity.
$Dinakara$ ($c.$ 1578) wrote three works. His $Candr\bar{a}rk\bar{i}$ deals with true place of the sun and moon in astronomy in 33 verses. His $Khe\d{t}asiddhi$ describes methods for finding the true place of the planets. He also wrote commentary to his own $Candr\bar{a}rk\bar{i}$.
$Acyuta$ $Pi\d{s}\bar{a}ra\d{t}i$ ($c.$ 1580), a non-Brahmin astronomer, mathematician of Kerala, was a student of $Jye\d{s}\d{t}hadeva$. He, for the first time in Indian astronomy, incorporated the correction in the $Sphu\d{t}anir\d{n}aya$ and explained its rational elaborately in his work, $R\bar{a}\acute{s}igolasphu\d{t}an\bar{i}ti$ (ed. by K. V. Sarma, Adyar Library, Madras, 1955). The correction was first introduced in Western astronomy by Tycho Brahe at about the same time. His $Kara\d{n}ottama$ (ed. by Raghavan Pillai, TSS 213, Trivendrum, 1964) deals with astronomical computation. He wrote commentaries on $S\bar{u}ryasiddh\bar{a}nta$, $Ve\d{n}v\bar{a}roha$ of $M\bar{a}dhava$ and on his own $Sphu\d{t}anir\d{n}aya$ and $Kara\d{n}ottama$.
$Vi\acute{s}van\bar{a}tha$ $Daivaj\~{n}a$ (1580) son of $Div\bar{a}kara$ $Daivaj\~{n}a$ was a powerful teacher and commentator. He comes of an astronomer family which traces his ancestry back through a long line of astronomers in Maharashtra. He wrote as many as ten commentaries besides other works on almanacs. The works are $S\bar{u}ryasiddh\bar{a}ntod\bar{a}hara\d{n}a$ (commentary on the $S\bar{u}ryasiddh\bar{a}nta$), $Grahakautukod\bar{a}hara\d{n}a$, (commentary on the $Grahakautuka$ of $Ke\acute{s}ava$ $Daivaj\~{n}a$), $Grahal\bar{a}ghavod\bar{a}hara\d{n}a$ (commentary on the $Grahal\bar{a}ghava$ of $Ga\d{n}e\acute{s}a$ $Daivaj\~{n}a$), $Kara\d{n}akut\bar{u}halod\bar{a}hara\d{n}a$ (commentary on the $Kara\d{n}akut\bar{u}hala$ of $Bh\bar{a}skara$ II), $M\bar{a}kara\d{n}dod\bar{a}hara\d{n}a$ (commentary on the $Tithipatra$ of $Makaranda$), $R\bar{a}mavinodod\bar{a}hara\d{n}a$ (commentary on the $R\bar{a}mavinoda$ of $R\bar{a}ma$ $Daivaj\~{n}a$), $\acute{S}iromanyud\bar{a}hara\d{n}a$ (commentary on the $Siddh\bar{a}nta\acute{s}iroma\d{n}i$ of $Bh\bar{a}skara$ II), $Somasiddh\bar{a}nta\d{t}\bar{i}k\bar{a}$, $V\bar{a}si\d{s}\d{t}hasiddh\bar{a}nta\d{t}\bar{i}k\bar{a}$ and others.

The $Kara\d{n}apaddhati^{3a}$ (1596) is an important astronomical work in ten chapters written by anonymous Kerala Brahmin family of $\acute{S}ivapura^4$. The text is edited in Trivandrum Sanskrit series no. 126. Apart from usual elements and formulae characteristics of Hindu astronomy, the work gives the values of $pi$, sine, co-sine and tan series. This also used the idea of indeterminate equations to calculate $m\bar{a}h\bar{a}h\bar{a}ra$ and $mah\bar{a}gu\d{n}a$ required in connection with the calculation of the mean motions of planets.
$K\d{r}\d{s}\d{n}a$ ($c.$ 1600) was the son of $Ball\bar{a}la$ of Dadhighrāma (Vidarbha). He belonged to a prominent family of $jyauti\d{s}a$, patronised by the King of Vidarbha. The family moved from Vidarbha to Benaras in the sixteenth century. He was the student of $Vi\d{s}\d{n}u$ who was the pupil of $N\d{r}si\dot{m}ha$, the nephew of $Ga\d{n}e\acute{s}a$ of Nandigrāma and was patronised by Jahangir (1605-1627) according to his younger brother $Ra\dot{n}gan\bar{a}tha$. He wrote three works, viz. $B\bar{i}j\bar{a}\dot{n}kura$ (commentary on the $B\bar{i}jaga\d{n}ita$ of $Bh\bar{a}skara$ II), a commentary on the $J\bar{a}takapaddhati$ of $\acute{S}r\bar{i}pati$, and a commentary on the $L\bar{i}l\bar{a}vat\bar{i}$ of $Bh\bar{a}skara$ II.
$Div\bar{a}kara$ $Daivaj\~{n}a$ (1603), son of $N\d{r}si\dot{m}ha$ $Daivaj\~{n}a$, was the resident of Golagrāma (Maharashtra). He had a controversy with $Kamal\bar{a}kara$ and tried to refute some of the latter's contribution.

Rāma Daivajña (1615), son of Madhusūdana was the resident of Pārthapura. He wrote a commentary entitled *Yantracintāmaṇitīkā* (commentary on the *Yantracintāmaṇi* of Cakradhara).
Kamalākara (*c.* 1616-1700), son of Nṛsiṃha Daivajña, was the descendent of Viśvanātha family of Golagrāma. He was born in Benaras. His brothers, Divākara and Raṅganātha, were also noted astronomers in Benaras in mid-seventeenth century. He was the court astronomer of Jahangir (1605-1627). He composed his work *Siddhāntatattvaviveka* in 1658 A. D. He was a follower of *Sūryasiddhānta* and tried to refute some of the views of the Bhāskara II and Munīśvara. Though *Siddhāntatattvaviveka* is mainly a work on astronomy, he made contribution to trigonometry by giving several correct relations between chords and its corresponding arcs in his section of *Spaṣṭādhyāya*. Some of the passages of the *Siddhāntatattvaviveka* are evidently copied from the *Elements*. Other instances of resemblence can also be identified with particular propositions of *Elements*. On the whole, there is absolutely no doubt that Kamalākara had knowledge of Euclid's *Elements*. His other works are *Grahagolatattva*, *Grahasaraṇī*, *Kairasyudāharaṇa* (commentary on the *Līlāvatī* of Bhāskara II), *Manoramā* (commentary on the *Grahalāghava* of Gaṇeśa Daivajña), *Sauravāsanā* (commentary on the *Sūryasiddhānta*) and *Śeṣavāsanā*, a supplement to the author’s *Siddhānta-Tattvaviveka*. Kamalākara combined traditional Indian astronomy with elements of Aristotelian physics, Euclidean geometry and Ptolemaic astronomy as presented by Ulug Beg.
Malayendusūri (*c.* 1659) wrote a commentary on the *Yantrarāja* of Mahendrasūri (*c.* 1320). It is a guide to the preparation of an instrument by means of which the times of day and night may be accurately ascertained. The work in 5 chapters are *Ghaṭanā*, *Gaṇita*, *Yantraracanā*, *Yantraśodhana*, and *Vicāraṇa*. Malayendu Sūri wrote another work *Yantrarājaracanā* which is also useful in the preparation of *Yantrarāja*.
Jayasimha (died in 1667), the ablest General under emperor Aurangzeb (1659-1709), was also a great patron of learning. His descendent Sawai Jayasiṃha or Jayasiṃha II became the prince of Amber in Rajasthan and stabilised his Kingdom in 1708. The prince was greatly interested in mathematics particularly in astronomy. ## 4. TREND OF INDO-PERSIAN LITERATURE

The Persian and Arabic literature were produced mostly under the patronage of Mughal rulers. Many standard works were brought from outside India. Some of these are *Khulāṣatu'l Ḥisāb* of Bahā'ud-dīn 'Āmūlī (c. 1547-1621) written originally in Arabic in Iran, *Tahrīr-i-Uqlidas al Mijiṣṭī*—Arabic version of Euclid's *Elements*
92 A. K. BAG
and Ptolemy's *Almagest* by Naṣīru'd-din aṭ-ṭusi etc. besides some others on accountancy, which attracted attention of many Indian scholars.
Attempt has been made to translate and write commentaries on these texts. Similar attempts have been tried to make translations of *Bṛhatsaṃhitā*, *Līlāvatī*, and *Bījagaṇita* in the period, but very few attempts have been made to make a comparison with the available knowledge in Sanskrit sources. These were written mainly for readers of Persian who knew no other language and had no access in standard Sanskrit, Arabic and Persian treatises in mathematics. Only a partial attempt has been made by Munīśvara and Kamalākara, Jagannātha Paṇḍit and Rājā Jayasiṃha to make a synthesis of the available Indian knowledge and that of Ptolemy and Euclid. The real success has been achieved by Rājā Jayasiṃha who used the services of great Sanskrit scholars having knowledge of Sanskrit, Persian, Arabic and European works which helped him to modernize the observatories in Delhi, Jaipur, Mathura, Banaras, Ujjain and left two almanacs in Sanskrit and one work in Persian.[^7]

## 5. CONCLUSION

To sum up, the present survey gives an idea of the Indian literature on mathematics during the period. It is by no means complete. The account gives both Hindu and Islamic traditions in India as well as their activities in the form of writing commentaries on older Indian and some Persian texts, which helps us to some extent to assess the trend of literature. The activities of the Indian scholars in mathematics and astronomy also attracted the attention of European scholars, viz. Giovanni Dominique Cassini (1691-1699), Le Gentil (1772), Robert Barker (1777), Joseph Tieffenthaler (1785-1789), Bailey (1878), William Jones (1790), Samuel Devis (1790-1792) and John Bentley (1799), who tried to make an assessment of Indian activities by writing translation of the texts, writing articles in French, Latin, German language. Sen[^8] has made a resumé of these activities. But the assessment of the actual contribution in the period deserves more intensive research. This is very important because this will help us to assess correctly the proportion of cultural interdependence in the field of mathematical knowledge.
