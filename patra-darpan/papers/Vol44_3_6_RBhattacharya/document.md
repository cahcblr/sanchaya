# THE CIRCULAR DROṆA, KŪRMA AND SĀRATHACAKRA CITIS IN THE ŚULBASŪTRAS*

Very few details about the construction of the circular *citis* are available in the *Śulbasūtras*. The commentators too are of very little help. This paper deals with the three basic types of the circular *citis*: *Rathacakra* (without spokes), *Rathacakra* (with spokes), and *Paricāyya*/*Upacāyya*. It will be shown that the circular *Droṇa* and *Kūrma citis* are modelled on the first type, and the second and third types are interrelated. The second conclusion is that no circular *citi* was constructed by piling bricks; all of them are drawn (*parilikhita*) on the square *citis*.
In addition to the rectilinear fire altar (*citis*), the *Śulbasūtras* mention a number of circular *citis*. The *Baudhāyana Śulbasūtra* (BS) mentions the following: *Rathacakra citi* (chariot-wheel) of two types (with spokes and without spokes), and *Paricāyya*. Two other circular *citi*-s have both rectilinear and circular varieties, namely, *Droṇa* (trough) and *Kūrma* (tortoise). The *Āpastamba Śulbasūtra* (AS) refers to only one type of the chariot-wheel altar (without spokes) and merely mentions the circular variety of the trough altar; no details of its construction are provided. The tortoise altar, whether of the rectilinear or the circular variety, is absent. Along with the *Paricāyya* another name is given: *Upacāyya*, which is in all respects similar to the former; the only difference lies in the mode of arranging the bricks (clockwise is the former, anticlockwise is the latter). The *Mānava Śulbasūtra* (MS) mentions the chariot-wheel (with spokes) variety only; two variants (rectilinear and circular) of the trough and *Śmaśāna* altars are referred to but no details of either are given. The *Kātyāyana Śulbasūtra* (KS) merely records the name of the chariot-wheel altar, etc. and nothing else. The layouts of the circular *citis* designed by the commentators or editors show three basic types:
1. *Rathacakra* (without spokes)
2. *Rathacakra* (with spokes)
3. *Paricāyya*/*Upacāyya*
Can any inference be drawn on the basis of the above? It is evident from the text of the BS that no scriptural instruction was available regarding the *Rathacakra citi* and the circular variants of the *Droṇa* and *Kūrma citis* (5. 4, 6. 4, and 9. 3). One problem that the redactors of the *Śulbasūtras* faced is that the *Taittirīya Saṃhitā* (TS, 5. 4. 11) does not mention which kind of chariot wheel is intended to be made: with spokes or without spokes. Nor does it record the *Kūrma citi* at all. This is why the redactors of the BS had to resort to their own ingenuity. They first designed the *Rathacakra citi* (without spokes), and modelled the circular *Droṇa* and *Kūrma citis* on its basis. It seems that all the circular *citi*-s in the BS were designed by its redactors. A look at the *Rathacakara citi* (with spokes) would reveal that it is modelled on the *Paricāyya citi*.
As to the circular *citis*, the AS does not contain anything new. The *Rathacakra citi* (without spokes) is almost identical to the BS. The only name that the AS adds to the list of *citi*-s is the *Upacāyya* which is not found in the TS, 5. 4. 11 or any other *Śulbasūtras*. One wonders from where the redactors of the AS got it.

The first *sārathacakra citi* (with spokes) in the MS (3. 6-13) is different from the one described in the BS but it need not detain us, for the number of bricks does not conform to the prescribed number, 200, nor is the area confined to $7\frac{1}{2}$ *puruṣa* or 108, 000 sq. *aṅg*. The second form of the same *citi* is admitted to be “three times as large; in its circle the seven and a half square *puruṣas*, are hidden by the wise men” (3. 7. 1).[^1] The total number of bricks in all the five layers is 1768 (3. 7. 7) instead of the prescribed 1000. It is evident that there was no uniform reliable tradition of piling the circular altars to be followed. The redactors of the *Śulbasūtras* were, therefore, left to their own devices. Since the names of *citis* are there in the TS, something had to be done by the redactors of the *Śulbasūtra* for the benefit of the *adhvaryu*-s. The circular variants of the *Droṇa*, *Kūrma* and *Śmaśāna citis* seem to be mere figments of the redactors’ imagination or designed later by some priests.
However, there is another and more critical question to be resolved: Were the ‘special bricks’, i.e., bricks having one or two curved sides, fabricated by hand and then burnt in kilns, or all the circular *citis* were simply drawn on the square *citi*-s? The question is most relevant in relation to the *sārathacakra citi* which is apparently based on the *Paricāyya*/*Upacāyya citis*. Since no details of *Paricāyya*/*Upacāyya citis* are provided in the texts of the BS and AS, nothing definite can be said about them. The construction of the *Rathacakra citi* (without spokes) is not very difficult. Only a few ‘special bricks’ are required. The rest consists of rectilinear bricks. But when we come to *Sārathacakra citi* in the BS, the problem appears to be enormous.
There are reasons to believe from what the commentators of the BS write that no circular *citi* was actually constructed by piling bricks of different shapes and sizes; they were drawn (*parilikhita*) on the square base.
Let us look at what Dvārakānātha Yajvā and Vyaṅkaṭeśvara Dīkṣita, commentators on the BS, have to say about the construction of the *Rathacakra citi* (with spokes). Instead of translating the passages I quote below the summary version given by Sen and Bag:
> A chariot wheel with spokes consists of the central circular part, the nave, the spokes and the outer circular rim, the felly. The spokes connecting the nave and the felly alternate with empty spaces. In the procedure suggested, an area equivalent to the empty spaces between the spokes is first added and then removed. This area is here taken to be equivalent to that of 64

(Dvarakanath Yajva) explains, it is constructed out of the central square of 25 bricks by putting 4 poles, one each at the centre of the corner bricks, at $E, F, G$ and $H$, and then joining them. The square $EFGH$ forms the nave (*nābhi*).
In the same manner, another square $IJKL$ is formed of 144 bricks, centrally placed within $ABCD$. As D. explains, it is constructed out of the central square of 169 bricks, each side containing 13 bricks. A pole is placed at the centre of each of the four corner bricks, $I, J, K$ and $L$, and these poles are joined. The area between the squares $IJKL$ and $EFGH$ is equivalent to the area of 128 (144–16) bricks, half of which, that is, 64 is used for making the spokes (*arā*) and the remaining half (64 bricks) for the empty spaces (*vedi*).
The space between the squares $ABCD$ and $IJKL$, equivalent to 145 (289–144) bricks constitutes the felly (*nemi*).

Then three squares $ABCD$, $EFGH$ and $IJKL$ are turned into circles of radii $OM, OP$ and $ON$ respectively, according to the method given in rule 2. 9 [= 1. 58 in Thibaut]. The space between the felly and the nave, that is between the circles of radii $ON$ and $OP$ is radially divided into 32 equal parts and half of them, in alternate order, is removed. In this way, an area equivalent to that of 64 bricks is rejected, and the remaining area of the wheel with the nave, spokes and the felly is exactly equal to that of 225 bricks or $7\frac{1}{2}$ sq. *pu*.
The illustration shows that no curvilinear brick is employed; the circular shape is merely a matter of drawing. The illustrations provided by V. Bhattacharya and Kulkarni (2003) give the impression of the bricks at the felly having irregular shapes. But apparently no such bricks were actually made. All the circles were drawn. Hence the aphorism, *nābhim antataḥ parilikhet* (5. 20), “The nave is to be circumbscribed at its borders with a circle (i.e., the square forming the nave is to be turned into a circle).” The same statement holds true for the other circular altars. The BS teaches how a square having a given area is to be drawn by drawing a number of circles (*ālekhya*, 1. 22). Similarly it teaches how a circle equal in area to a given square is to be drawn (*parilikhet*, 1. 58). But building a three-dimensional altar with the help of bricks, both rectilinear and partly curvilinear, is a different proposition altogether. The illustrations of the *Dhiṣṇya* (2. 39, 40) and *Mārjālīyam* (2. 41) denote two-dimensional figures, whereas the fire altars—each of it having five layers—are knee-high constructions requiring actual bricks. It is too much to expect that the priests of the Yajurvedic times could go beyond rectilinear bricks, whether quadrilateral or pentalateral or hexalateral or their derivatives, to build circular fire altars. So, it is apparent that all circular altars are merely circles drawn over square altars. In other words, the circular altars are purely make-believe.
Such a conclusion is borne out by the description of the *Samūhya* and *Paricāyya* altars. The BS summarily disposes of the two by saying : “[They] are explained by the former construction in the shape of a wheel” (7. 13). Vyaṅkaṭeśvara Dīkṣita in his comments on the next aphorism uses the word *lekhaniyaḥ*, ‘to be drawn’.[^3] Similarly in course of explaining BS, 7. 14-16, Dikshita and Yajva use the words *likhitvā*, *likhitāsu*, and *lekhaniyāḥ*.[^4] The root *pari-likh* in the *Śulbasūtras* always refer to drawing or describing a circle (Figs. 2-6). There is no guarantee that Dikshita is right, for there is no evidence in his commentary to show that he had actual experience of piling any of the fire altars mentioned in the *BS*, rectilinear or curvilinear, or even having seen such altars being piled. Dikshita flourished as late as the seventeenth century AD and Yajva cannot be dated with certainty, except that he appeared after Āryabhaṭa (b. 476 AD). Hence their interpretations cannot be said to reflect the actual intention and practice prevalent in the Yajurvedic times. Even the *Śulbasūtras* themselves are so far removed in time from the *TS* that it is futile to find a linear continuation of the same tradition.
That this conclusion is not a mere conjecture is borne out by the use of the word, *aviśeṣāt*, ‘since no particular is given’, which occurs thrice in the *BS*, all in connection with the circular altars, namely, the chariot-wheel (without spokes), trough and tortoise altars (*BS*, 3. 182, 218 and 272 in Thibaut; 5. 4, 6. 4, and 9. 3 in Kulkarni (2003)). Not that the word, *aviśeṣāt* and its derivatives do not occur in other contexts, but they do not refer to brick-built altars.
In the light of the preceding observations it is pretty obvious that the chariot-wheel altar (without spokes), the circular trough, tortoise and *Śmaśāna* altars are the products of the imagination of the redactors of the *Śulbasūtras*. It is evident that the commentators too provide their instructions on the basis of what they could infer from the text.[^5]
