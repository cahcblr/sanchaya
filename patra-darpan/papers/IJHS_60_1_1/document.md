# An *Uyirêḻuttu* or *svara*-based system of numeration used in Tamil astronomy texts

**Abstract**
Indian texts of astronomy and mathematics use systems of numeration where words or sequences of syllables are used to denote pure numbers or values of astronomical quantities such as the longitude etc. Three such systems, namely the *bhūta-saṅkhyā* system, the *Āryabhaṭīya* system and the *kaṭapayādi* system, are well known and have been widely discussed in the literature. In this article, we shall discuss a novel and ingenious vowel (*uyirêḻuttu* in Tamil, *svara* in Sanskrit) based system of numeration which has been used in the late 12th or 13th century Tamil astronomy text, *Cūḍāmaṇi Uḷḷamuḍaiyān*, of Tirukkoṭṭiyūr Nambi. We show that using this numeration system, *Cūḍāmaṇi Uḷḷamuḍaiyān* presents what is perhaps the earliest available account of the *vākya* system for computing the longitude of the moon. We also indicate how the same system has been employed in later Tamil texts of astronomy and in the formulation of the 248 *Candravākyas* in Tamil, which are referred to as the *Pañcāṅgavākyas*.

## 1 Introduction

India is famous for its pioneering contribution to the development of the decimal place-value system of representation of numbers, where each number is represented as a sequence of the ten digits, 1, 2, ...9, and 0. The seminal works of Āryabhaṭa (c. 499 CE), Brahmagupta (c. 628) and Bhāskara I (c. 629 CE) make use of the decimal place-value representation with zero to describe all the standard algorithms for performing various arithmetical operations with numbers.
Apart from using this representation in texts and inscriptions, Indians also developed systems of numeration where words, phrases or sequences of syllables are used to denote numbers. Three such systems, namely the *bhūta-saṅkhyā* system, the *Āryabhaṭīya* system, and the *kaṭapayādi* system are well known and have been widely discussed in the literature.
These systems were used extensively in all the major texts of astronomy and mathematics composed in the metrical verse form, which enabled these texts to be committed to memory by all students and scholars. These numeration schemes were also used in various prose commentaries of these texts. Such representations were also used to develop mnemonics for remembering tables of sine values, astronomical parameters and many other mathematical and astronomical quantities of interest. In particular, the *kaṭapayādi* system was employed for encoding various intermediate sequences of values which were used in astronomical computations in the so-called *vākya* (sentence or phrase) system of astronomy. It was often the case that these *vākyas* were also meaningful as sentences or linguistic phrases.
Before considering the novel and ingenious *uyirêḻuttu* or *svara*-based system of numeration used in Tamil astronomical texts, we shall briefly describe the three well-known systems of numeration listed above. For more details, we refer the readers to the detailed accounts found in Datta and Singh (1935, pp. 53–75) and Sarma (2009, 2012), along with the references cited therein. We shall employ the standard IAST system of transliteration of Sanskrit words and the transliteration scheme shown below (Table 1) for Tamil words and passages.

### 1.1 *Bhūta-Saṅkhyā* system of numeration

From the Vedic times, Indians developed a decimal system of nomenclature of numbers, which was a precursor of the decimal place-value system. The Vedic corpus and other ancient texts assigned a name to each power of ten, apart from the names of numbers from 1 to 100, and used them to name every number in a systematic manner.
The idea of *bhūta-saṅkhyā* system was to employ word-names for the digits from 1 to 9 and zero and sometimes also for larger numbers such as 12, 25, etc. For this, use was made of names of objects or concepts with a fixed number associated with them either in nature or our tradition. For instance, the words for eyes or hands do get naturally associated with the number 2. In the same way, Vedas will be associated with 4, *ṛtus* (seasons) will be associated with 6. The list goes on, and one can easily gauge that there is a huge wealth of words referring to objects that we can experience through our senses and those that refer to objects and concepts in our civilisational memory, and both provide us with a large corpus of *bhūta-saṅkhyā* words.
The use of such words for denoting numbers can be seen occasionally in the Vedic corpus but more often in the *Sūtra* and other post-Vedic literature. *Śrauta-sūtras* of Kātyāyana and Lāṭyāyana use words like *Gāyatrī* (a Vedic metre with 24 syllables in all) to denote the number 24 and *Jagatī* (metre with 48 syllables in all) for 48. The *Vedāṅgajyotiṣa* of Lagadha uses words like *rūpa* for 1, *bhasamūha* (the collection of *nakṣatras*) for 27 etc. Such word names for numbers are used extensively in the *Chandaḥ-sūtra* of Piṅgala, which incidentally discusses many of the combinatorial aspects of organising and counting metres of a given number of syllables.
The real emergence of a “*bhūta-saṅkhyā* system” of numeration goes back at least to the famous text of astronomy, *Pañcasiddhāntikā*, composed by Varāhamihira (c.550 CE) in the sixth century of the common era. Here, the *bhūta-saṅkhyā* words are used in conjunction with the place-value system to denote all possible numbers. The convention adopted is that a sequence of *bhūta-saṅkhyā* words corresponds to the number which is obtained by writing the numbers associated with successive *bhūta-saṅkhyā* words in the sequence, in the same order, from right to left. This convention is normally stated in the form “*aṅkānāṁ vāmato gatiḥ*” [the digits proceed (from the right) to the left].
To illustrate this “*bhūta-saṅkhyā* system”, we cite the verse 2.2 from the *Pañcasiddhāntikā* which gives the first step in the computation of the longitude of the moon in the *Vasiṣṭhasiddhānta* (Sastri & Sarma, 1993, p. 27):
> रसगुणनवेन्दुयुक्ते
> शशिगुणखगुणो-
> -द्धृते घना द्युुगणे।
> शेषे नवभिर्गुणिते गतयो
> -ऽष्टजिनैः पदं शेषम्॥
> *rasaguṇanavenduyukte*
> *śaśiguṇakhaguṇo-*
> *-ddhṛte ghanā dyugaṇe ।*
> *śeṣe navabhirguṇite gatayo*
> *’ṣṭajinaiḥ padaṁ śeṣam॥*
Add 1936 (*rasaguṇanavendu*) to the number of days elapsed since the epoch (*dyugaṇa*) and divide by 3031 (*śaśiguṇakhaguṇa*), and the result is called the *ghana*. The remainder (in the above division) is multiplied by 9 (*nava*) and divided by 248 (*aṣṭajina*). The quotient (in the above division) is called *gati*, and the remainder is called the *pada*.

In the above verse, *rasa* stands for 6 (the well-known *ṣaḍrasas* or the six flavours), *guṇa* stands for 3 (the well-known *triguṇas* or the three qualities), *nava* is, of course, 9, and *indu* stands for 1 (the moon). Just to provide one more illustration for the *bhūta-saṅkhyā* system of numeration, we cite the following famous verse due to Mādhava (the illustrious founder of the Kerala School of Astronomy), which Nīlakaṇṭha cites in his *Āryabhaṭīyabhāṣya* as a very good approximation for the value of $\pi$ (Sastri, 1930, p. 42):
> विबुधनेत्रगजााहिहुताशन-
> -त्रिगुणवेदभवाारणबााहवः ।
> नवनिखर्वमिते वृृतिविस्तरे
> परिधिमानमिदं जगदुर्बुुधाः ॥
> *Vibudhanetragajāhihutāśana-*
> *-triguṇavedabhavāraṇabāhavaḥ ।*
> *navanikharvamite vṛtivistare*
> *paridhimānamidaṁ jagadurbudhāḥ ॥*
The experts have stated that for a circle of diameter $9 \times 10^{11}$ (*navanikharva*), the measure of the circumference would be 2,827,433,388,233 (*vibudhanetragajāhihutāśanatriguṇavedabhavāraṇabāhavaḥ*).
In the above verse, *vibudha* stands for 33 (the 33 *devatas*), *netra* for 2 (the 2 eyes), *gaja* for 8 (the *aṣṭadiggajas* eight elephants guarding the directions), *ahi* for 8 (the *aṣṭanāgas* the eight serpents), *hutāśana* for 3 (the three sacrificial fires), *tri* is 3, *guṇa* stands for 3 (the three qualities), *veda* stands for 4 (the four Vedas), *bha* stands for 27 (the twenty-seven *nakṣatras*), *vāraṇa* stands for 8 (the eight elephants) and *bāhavaḥ* for 2 (the two arms). Following the convention ‘*aṅkānāṁ vāmato gatiḥ*’ meaning ‘numbers proceed (from the right) to left’ the phrase *vibudhanetragajāhihutāśanatriguṇavedabhavāraṇabāhavaḥ* actually denotes the number 2827433388233. If we divide this circumference by the diameter $9 \times 10^{11}$ (*navanikharva*) we obtain a value of $\pi$ accurate to 11 decimal places.

### 1.2 *Āryabhaṭīya* system of numeration

The *Āryabhaṭīya* system of numeration is a unique invention of Āryabhaṭa, one of the foremost astronomers of ancient India. The *Āryabhaṭīya* (c.499 CE) was written prior to the works of Varāhamihira, and it does not use the *bhūta-saṅkhyā* system of numeration. Instead, it uses a syllable-based numeration system introduced in the second verse of the *Daśagītikā* section. We refer the reader to Shukla and Sarma (1976, pp. 3–5) for the verse, its translation and explanation.
The main feature of the *Āryabhaṭīya* system of numeration is that the *vargīya-vyañjanas* (the consonants क् to म्) are assigned the values 1, 2, ..., 25; and the *avargīya-vyañjanas* (य् to ह्) are assigned the values 30, 40, ..., 80 as listed in the Table 2 below. The *svaras* or the vowels attached to these consonants in any syllable indicate that these numbers have to be placed in different even place-value positions (associated with even powers of 10) as indicated in the Table 3 below (note that the long and short vowels indicate the same place value).
To illustrate the *Āryabhaṭīya* system of numeration, we shall consider the initial portion of the very next verse 3 of *Daśagītikā*, where the revolution numbers of the sun and the moon are stated (Shukla & Sarma, 1976, p. 6):
> युगरविभगणाः ख्युघृशशि चयगियिङुशुछ्लृ
> *yugaravibhagaṇāḥ khyughṛ śaśi cayagiyiṅuśuchlṛ*
The revolutions in a yuga of the sun are 4,320,000 (*khyughṛ*); of the moon 57,753,336 (*cayagiyiṅuśuchlṛ*).
In the above verse, *khy* stands for $2 + 30 = 32$, and *khyu* for $32 \times 10^4$; and *gh* stands for 4, and *ghṛ* for $4 \times 10^6$. Hence, the phrase *khyughṛ* denotes the number $32 \times 10^4 + 4 \times 10^6 = 4,320,000$. In the same way, *ca* stands for $6 \times 10^0$, *ya* for $30 \times 10^0$, *gi* for $3 \times 10^2$, *yi* for $30 \times 10^2$, *ṅu* for $5 \times 10^4$, *śu* for $70 \times 10^4$, *chlṛ* for $(7+50) \times 10^6$. Hence, the phrase *cayagiyiṅuśuchlṛ* denotes the number $6 \times 10^0 + 30 \times 10^0 + 3 \times 10^2 + 30 \times 10^2 + 5 \times 10^4 + 70 \times 10^4 + 57 \times 10^6 = 57,753,336$.

Āryabhaṭa used this scheme throughout the astronomical treatise *Āryabhaṭīya*. It is noteworthy that even though almost all the later astronomers have referred to the *Āryabhaṭīya*, they have not used his numeration scheme, perhaps because many of the linguistic phrases denoting various numbers that appear in the *Āryabhaṭīya* are indeed very difficult to pronounce. Further, most of these phrases do not carry any linguistic value or meaning. So even though metrical compositions are feasible, ascertaining any lingual or literary meanings to the verse is not. Notwithstanding this feature, *Āryabhaṭīya* appears to be the earliest text to have used syllables for representing numbers. It is very likely that this system would have inspired the later schemes using syllables or linguistic phrases to represent numbers.

### 1.3 *Kaṭapayādi* system of numeration

The *kaṭapayādi* system of numeration can be clearly traced back to the 7th century Kerala astronomical works, *Grahacāranibandhana* (c.683) of Haridatta and *Karaṇaratna* (c.689) of Devācārya. There is also the view that Vararuci, the author of the famous *Candravākyas* which are encoded in the *kaṭapayādi* system, perhaps lived in the 4th/5th century. The *kaṭapayādi* system of numeration is a syllabic system where the consonants stand for the digits 1, 2, ...9, 0, as shown in Table 4. When there is more than one consonant in a syllable, the syllable stands for the digit associated with the consonant immediately prior to the vowel. Stand-alone vowels stand for 0, and stand-alone consonants (not linked to a vowel) are to be ignored. Finally, the number associated with a phrase is obtained by arranging all the digits sequentially from right to left, following the rule, “*aṅkānāṁ vāmato gatiḥ*” (the digits move from right to the left).

We cite the following definition of the *kaṭapayādi* system as it appears in the Kerala work *Sadratnamālā* (1819 CE) by Śaṅkaravarman (Madhavan, 2011, p. 10):
> नञावचश्च शून्याानि संख्यााःः कटपयाादयःः ।
> मिश्रेे तूूपान्त्यहल्संंख्याा न च चिन्त्योो हलस्वरःः ॥
> *nañāvcaśca śūnyāni saṅkhyāḥ kaṭapayādayaḥ ।*
> *miśre tūpāntyahalsaṅkhyā na ca cintyo halasvaraḥ ॥*
The न (*na*) and ञ (*ña*) and the vowels stand for zero. The consonants beginning with क (*ka*), ट (*ṭa*), प (*pa*) and य (*ya*), stand for the corresponding digits (1, 2, 3, ...). In the case of conjunct consonants, the digit associated with the penultimate one (preceding the vowel) is to be taken. A (stand-alone) consonant without any vowel is to be ignored.
An important feature of the *kaṭapayādi* system of numeration is that, most often, the syllabic phrases employed to denote numbers also have a linguistic meaning. We shall illustrate the use of *kaṭapayādi* system in the renowned work *Grahacāranibandhana* (c.683 CE) of Haridatta. Haridatta was the originator of the *parahita* system, which was essentially an improvement and simplification of the *Āryabhaṭīya* and was adopted at the 12-year festival held at Tirunāvāya on the banks of the Bhāratapuḻa river in Kerala. In the verses 2–6 of the first Chapter of *Grahacāranibandhana*, while describing the method of computing the *ahargaṇa*, or the number of days elapsed since the epoch, Haridatta uses the following linguistic phrases to denote the corresponding numbers indicated in the parenthesis (Sarma, 1954, pp. 1–2): युद्ध, *yuddha* (91); अनूनज्ञानतुङ्ग, *anūnajñānatuṅga* (3,600,000); काल, *kāla* (31); शं, *śaṁ* (7); दधिकााम, *dadhikāma* (5198); सुु, *su* (7); and निरयोोद्धृत, *nirayoddhṛta* (69120).
In the first phrase *yuddha*, the syllable *yu* stands for 1 and *ddha* for 9 and hence, *yuddha* denotes the number 91. In the second phrase, *anūnajñānatuṅga*, the syllables *a*, *nū*, *na*, *jñā*, and *na*, all stand for 0; the syllable *tu* stands for 6 and *ṅga* for 3, so that the phrase *anūnajñānatuṅga* stands for 3,600,000. We can similarly analyse the other phrases and arrive at the numbers they stand for. Notice that all the syllabic phrases used here are meaningful.
The *kaṭapayādi* system of numeration has been extensively used in the astronomical works from Kerala and also in several works written in Tamil Nadu—such as the works of Sūryadevayajvan (c. 1250), the canonical text of the *vākya* system, *Vākyakaraṇa* (c. 1282–1316), and its commentary by Sundararāja (c. 1500). This system indeed became so popular in south India that it was used to name the *melakarta-rāgas* (standard scales) of Carnatic music so that the first two letters of the name indicate the index number associated with the *rāga* so that the notes that occur in the *melakarta-rāga* can be inferred straight away from its name (Ramanathan & Pai, 2020, pp. 1135–1138).
The *kaṭapayādi* system is specially employed in the *vākya* system of astronomy, where most of the intermediate parameters and constants as well as the computed tables of true longitudes for given periods, are expressed in terms of *vākyas* or linguistic phrases, which often are so constructed as to be meaningful. Given that these numeration schemes can be subjected to automation, exploring the *vākya* system can be rendered seamlessly (Anusha et al., 2017, pp. 588–591). We shall illustrate this with the case of the famous *Candravākyas* of Vararuci, which give the true longitudes of the moon over a period of 248 days, which is roughly equal to 9 anomalistic periods or periods of successive conjunctions of the moon and its apogee. If $\lambda$ is the longitude of the moon when it is coincident with its apogee on some day at some time, then the successive *vākyas* give the increase in the true longitude of the moon from $\lambda$ for the succeeding days at the same time. We give here the first three *Candravākyas* along with the corresponding increments in the longitude of the moon from its initial value (Sastri & Sarma, 1962, p.125):
> गीीर्नःः श्रेेयःः *gīrnaḥ śreyaḥ* ($0^r$ 12° 03')
> धेेनवःः श्रीःः *dhenavaḥ śrīḥ* ($0^r$ 24° 09')
> रुद्रस्तुुनम्यःः *rudrastu namyaḥ* ($1^r$ 06° 22')
The three superscripts used above, in the case of numbers, denote the *rāśis* (signs), *aṁśas* (degrees) and *kalās* (minutes), respectively. Now, if the true longitudes of the moon and its apogee happen to be the same and given by $\lambda$ on someday at some time, then according to the above *vākyas*, the true longitude of the moon on the next three days at the same time will be given by $\lambda + 12^\circ 03'$, $\lambda + 24^\circ 09'$ and $\lambda + 36^\circ 22'$.
As we can easily see, the above *vākyas* constitute meaningful sentences, making memorising them easier. It can verily be said that the *kaṭapayādi* system of numeration is at the very heart of the *vākya* system of astronomy. For more details on the development of *vākya* system, we refer the reader to Pai et al., (2017, especially Appendices D, E) and Sriram (2022).

## 2 *Uyirêḻuttu* or *svara*-based system of numeration used in *Cūḍāmaṇi Uḷḷamuḍaiyān*

While the *Āryabhaṭīya* system of numeration is not found in any other known text of Indian astronomy or mathematics, the *bhūta-saṅkhyā* system was widely employed after the seventh century of the common era in the texts and even inscriptions “through-out India and also in South-East Asia” (Sarma, 2012). The *kaṭapayādi* system, which seems to have been first used in texts from Kerala in the seventh century, became a very popular system being used in the Sanskrit astronomical texts written both in Kerala as well as Tamil Nadu. Sarma (2012) has noted the use of this system in Sanskrit and regional language texts written in other regions. In any case, it was the main system in use in all the *parahita* texts and the *vākya* astronomy systems.
The *kaṭapayādi* system could be easily incorporated in texts of all regional languages of India with occasional minor modifications, except in Tamil. This is because the Tamil language, from the very ancient period, does not use the aspirated or *mahāprāṇa* consonant sounds (such as *kha*, *gha*, etc.). Further, the language’s written version does not use separate symbols or letters for the voiced consonants (*ga*, *ja*, etc.) among the *vargīya-vyañjanas*. When the *parahita* system of astronomy became popular in Tamil Nadu, it seems to have led to the invention of an entirely new numeration system exploiting the Tamil language’s vowels. The use of vowels in numeration does indeed go back to the *Āryabhaṭīya*, which used them as place-value markers. The numeration system developed in Tamil Nadu was a much simpler syllable-based system that assigned numerical values exclusively to the vowels or *svaras*, called *uyirêḻuttu* in Tamil. One of the early texts where one gets to see this scheme in execution is in a late twelfth or thirteenth-century jyotiṣa text called *Cūḍāmaṇi Uḷḷamuḍaiyān* by Tirukkoṭṭiyūr Nambi. The novel *uyirêḻuttu*, or *svara*-based system of numeration, is used in the penultimate or the eighth chapter of the Text, called *Parahita Gaṇitam*, which the author says has been written based on the texts written in Sanskrit. The same numeration system is used in the ninth chapter titled *Sayantamālai*, which deals with eclipse computations, which are again said to be based on Sanskrit texts.

We shall first describe the Tamil vowel-based system of numeration and then illustrate its use in the *Cūḍāmaṇi Uḷḷamuḍaiyān*. Whereas the vowels have no numeric values in the *kaṭapayādi* system, in the vowel-based numeration system, the vowels are of paramount importance, and the consonants are not assigned any numeric value. Briefly, the vowel-number mapping is done, as shown in Table 5. One important feature to note in Table 5 is that Tamil (like the other south Indian languages) has also the short vowels *ê* (எ) and *ô* (ஒ), apart from the long vowels *e* (ஏ) and *o* (ஓ). The latter two are similar to the ए and ओ used in Sanskrit and the north Indian languages.
The rules of this vowel-based numeration system are as follows:
(i) Each vowel, whether it appears independently or with a consonant, will code for the number as given in Table 5.
(ii) Independent consonants that are not associated with any vowel are to be ignored.
(iii) The numbers associated with the successive individual syllables in a linguistic phrase are to be placed from right to left in order to get the number denoted by the phrase.
The working method of this scheme may be easily understood with the help of a few examples. Consider the Tamil word *parahitam*. The syllables in this word are ‘*pa, ra, hi, ta* and *m*’ where all the syllables except the last are associated with a vowel. The numbers they stand for are 1, 1, 3 and 1, respectively. Thus, the number denoted by the phrase *parahitam* is 1311, adopting the convention of reading the numbers from right to left. Similarly, in the word *uḷḷamuḍaiyān*, we have the following syllables ‘*u, ḷḷa, mu, ḍai, yā*, and *n*’ which stand for 5, 1, 5, 9 and 2, respectively, where the last syllable is not considered as it is a stand-alone consonant. So, the number denoted by the phrase *uḷḷamuḍaiyān* is 29,515.
It is important to mention that readers must not confuse this numeration scheme with the alpha-numeral system of representing numbers used in Tamil script, as shown in Table 6. This way of writing numbers in the Tamil script has been in vogue from ancient times, like the Roman system of numerals. Currently, these numerals are mostly used as page numbers and in paper currency notes from countries like Mauritius. This alpha-numeral system has no connection with the vowel-based numeration system used in Tamil astronomy texts.

The verse 6 of Hoisington (1848, p. 23) and verse 380 of Kamesvaran (2007, pp. 171–172) give the method of computing the *suddadina*, which is the *ahargaṇa* or the number of days elapsed since the beginning of Kaliyuga till the beginning of the current (solar) year. The *vākya* process of computing the true longitude of the moon for any given day is described in verses 13–14 of Hoisington (1848, pp. 27–28) and verses 382–383 of Kamesvaran (2007, pp. 172–173). As we shall see below, the verse 382 of the Satyabhama Kamesvaran edition is defective. However, between them, we can clearly recognise the following *vākya* method of computing the longitude of the moon for any desired day (*iṣṭadina*).
For this, one starts with the *suddadina* or the number of days elapsed from the beginning of Kaliyuga till the beginning of the desired year, to which the number of days elapsed till the given day is added. This gives the number of days elapsed from the beginning of Kaliyuga till the given day. From this is subtracted 15,65,411 (called the *śodhyadina* or *khaṇḍa* in later Sanskrit texts), which is a nearby day on which the longitudes of the moon and its apogee coincide at the time of sunrise. The residue (called *śodhyaśeṣa* or *khaṇḍaśeṣa* in later Sanskrit texts) is divided by 3,031. The remainder obtained in this division is then divided by 248. The remainder obtained in this last division gives the serial number of the *vākya* (among the 248 *Pañcāṅgavākyas*) which is to be used in the computation. To obtain the longitude of the moon, we are then asked to do the following: (i) Take the product of the quotient obtained in the division by 248, with $27^\circ 44'$. (ii) From the above result, subtract the product of the quotient obtained in the division by 3,031, with $22^\circ 29'$. Here, $22^\circ 29' = 12^r - 11^r 07^\circ 31'$, is the negative of the *dhruva* associated with the divisor 3031 (iii) From the result of the subtraction, further subtract $5^r 00^\circ 17' = 12^r - 06^r 29^\circ 43'$, where $06^r 29^\circ 43'$, is the true longitude associated with the Kali day 15,65,411. The result obtained in this step is called the *vākkiyadduruvam* (*vākyadhruva*). (iv) To this *vākkiyadduruvam* is added the longitude given by the *vākya* associated with the serial number found as the remainder in the division by 248. This gives the true longitude of the moon at sunrise on the desired day.
We may note that the above procedure is indeed a precursor of the more sophisticated procedure given in the later Sanskrit text, *Vākyakaraṇa* in verses I.9–13 (Sastri & Sarma, 1962, pp. 19–21). *Vākyakaraṇa* uses a larger *śodhyadina* 16,00,984. It also instructs that the *śodhyaśeṣa* should first be divided by 12,372. This divisor is used because 12,372 days are very nearly equal to 449 anomalistic revolutions of the moon. The rest of the procedure is the same as described in *Cūḍāmaṇi Uḷḷamuḍaiyān*, namely divisions by 3031 and 248 and so on, with essentially the same constants for multiplying the resultant quotients. Only the longitude associated with Kali day 16,00,984 and the longitude that should be used to multiply the quotient obtained in the division by 12,372 are the new parameters that have been introduced. *Vākyakaraṇa* also presents a correction to take account the fact that 248 days and 3031 days do not exactly correspond to 9 and 110 anomalistic revolutions of the moon.
We have discussed the *vākya* procedure for computing the longitude of the moon as presented in Chapter 8 of *Cūḍāmaṇi Uḷḷamuḍaiyān* mainly to show the importance of this text, as it seems to be the earliest available text which discusses this procedure. We should, of course, keep in mind the fact that the author of *Cūḍāmaṇi Uḷḷamuḍaiyān* declares at the outset that he is describing the methods of computation outlined in the earlier Sanskrit texts composed by great scholars. We are yet to come across any of these Sanskrit texts on the *vākya* method, so far.

In this way convert the numbers into sentences (*vākkiyaṅgaḷ*). In these sentences (*vākkyas*) the consonants (with no vowel attached), which appear either in the end or in the middle, have been included for the sake of pleasant tonality of the sound (while pronouncing the sentence) and hence they are not part of the signs that denote numbers.

The final verse of the text mentions that the work was written in the *Viḷambi* year corresponding to Śaka 1100 or 1178 CE. It also gives some details regarding the author, Tirukkoṭṭiyūr Nambi. Though this verse mentions that the text was composed in Śaka 1100 (1178 CE), there are some indications in the text that it might have been composed a few decades later than 1178 CE. First is the *śodhyadina* of “*mannanvīḍusūḻkuḷam*” or 15,65,411 mentioned in verse 13 of Hoisington which is also verse 382 in Satyabhama Kamesvaran, which we cited above, which deals with the computation of the longitude of the moon. The verse states that this *śodhyadina* is to be subtracted from the number of days elapsed since the beginning of Kaliyuga till the desired day for which the computations are to be made. This would mean that the text is concerned with computations for days whose *suddadina* or *ahargaṇa* from the beginning of the Kaliyuga is larger than 15,65,411—implying that the text is composed sometime later than 1185 CE. There is another verse (verse 16 of Chapter 9 in Hoisington (1848, pp. 30–31), which is also the verse 388 in Kamesvaran (2007, p. 176)), which gives a larger *śodhyadina*, “*sūḷppondortêṟkekuḷam*” or 15,87,006, which is to be subtracted from the *suddadina* or *ahargaṇa* for computing the longitude of planets. This would imply that the text is composed sometime later than 1244 CE. In view of these different date-markers we are not in a position to fix the time of composition of the text exactly. However, since this text does not use any of the *vākya* methods for planets, which are discussed in the famous Sanskrit text *Vākyakaraṇa*, which uses a later *śodhyadina* (16,00,984) and has been estimated to have been composed sometime during 1282–1316 CE (Sastri and Sarma 1962 p. 9), we can conclude that *Cūḍāmaṇi Uḷḷamuḍaiyān* was composed sometime in the last quarter of the 12th or the first half of the thirteenth century.

## 3 Use of the *Uyirêḻuttu* or *Svara*-based system of numeration in Tamil *Candravākyas* or *Pañcāṅgavākyas*

While discussing the *kaṭapayādi* system of numeration, we referred to the crucial role they play in the formulation of the famous *Candravākyas* of Vararuci, which present the true longitude of the moon for a period of 248 days starting from a day on which the longitude of the moon and its apogee coincide at some time. While discussing the *uyirêḻuttu* or *svara*-based system of numeration in Tamil, we also cited the verses in *Cūḍāmaṇi Uḷḷamuḍaiyān*, which refer to the method of computing these *vākyas*, and also describe the process by which the relevant *vākya* for a given day is to be identified. This would mean that the text is referring to a set of *vākyas* in Tamil, which encode the longitude values similar to those given by the Sanskrit *Candravākyas* of Vararuci.
It so happens that the editions of *Cūḍāmaṇi Uḷḷamuḍaiyān* of Mudaliyar (1861), Desikar (1927) and Kamesvaran (2007) do not give any such list of the 248 *vākyas*. Hoisington’s book does present a list of 248 Tamil *vākyas*, referred to as *Pañcāṅgavākyas* (Hoisington, 1848, pp. 88–91), which are encoded using the *uyirêḻuttu* or *svara*-based system of numeration and denote the same set of longitude values that are encoded in the Sanskrit *Candravākyas* of Vararuci. Since Hoisington’s book also deals with later texts, we do not know whether the *Pañcāṅgavākyas* listed in his book were the ones in use at the time of *Cūḍāmaṇi Uḷḷamuḍaiyān*.
In fact, we have so far come across only two other published lists of Tamil *Candravākyas* or *Pañcāṅgavākyas*, both of which use the same *uyirêḻuttu* or *svara*-based system of numeration. Margalinga Jotishar has published one such list at the end of his edition of the Tamil *Jyotiṣa* work *Vīmesura Uḷḷamuḍaiyān* (Jotishar, 1933, pp. 261–264). In this context, it may be noted that *Vīmesura Uḷḷamuḍaiyān* is a *Jyotiṣa* work which was composed much later than *Cūḍāmaṇi Uḷḷamuḍaiyān* in the Kali year 4728 or 1627 CE (Verse 10, Jotishar, 1933, p. 20). It also uses the *uyirêḻuttu* or *svara*-based system of numeration while discussing the *vākya* method of computation of the longitude of moon (Verses 11–18 in Jotishar., 1933, pp. 20–24). Another published list of *Candravākyas* or *Pañcāṅgavākyas* is found in the late nineteenth-century Tamil work *Jotiṣagaṇitaśāstiram*, which we referred to earlier, by Munampannai Krishnajosyar (1897, pp. 126–135). We see that the lists given in the editions of Krishnajosyar and Margalinga Jotishar are mostly the same, except for a few minor variations. However, the Tamil *Pañcāṅgavākyas* given in Hoisington’s book are indeed very different, though they also denote the same longitude values.

## 4 Conclusion

In summary, we reiterate that this currently not well-known Tamil *uyirêḻuttu* or *svara*-based numeration system was indigenously developed for use in the Tamil astronomical texts as the *kaṭapayādi* system is not suitable for Tamil. Our discussion has shown that this numeration system is indeed as robust and flexible as the *kaṭapayādi* system.
