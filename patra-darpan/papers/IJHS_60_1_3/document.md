**Abstract**  
This research paper looks into the scientific legacy of the astronomical observatory of the University of Kerala spread over the last two centuries. In the first 100 years of its existence as a scientific institution of a princely state, the observatory’s contributions benefited the global scientific community and resulted in acclaimed publications. After a century of its incarnation, the observatory underwent a series of ownership changes, resulting in changes in its visionary course. It became a part of the University of Kerala in 1976, where today’s observatory serves as an amateur center for popularizing astronomy. The growing city has left the observatory amidst a plethora of challenges, and it continues to strive to be a beacon for research and learning in astronomy and astrophysics on the southern peninsula of India.
**Keywords** Astronomy · Observatory · Trivandrum · Comet

## 1 Background

In 1829, at the age of 16, Prince Swathi Thirunal Rama Varma ascended the throne of Travancore (Menon, 1878). Today, the Travancore kingdom is a part of the state of Kerala, with its capital being Thiruvananthapuram. The Travancore kingdom’s capital was spelled “Trevandrum” in the official British records. The anglicized name Trivandrum for the capital city was the district’s official English name until 1991. In the early 1830s, the young king met John Caldecott, an Englishman working as a commercial agent for the Government of Travancore at Allapuzha. John Caldecott used to make astronomical observations with several portable instruments of his own.[^1] Caldecott’s descriptions of his observations of the various movements of the heavenly bodies fascinated the young king. Maharaja Swathi Thirunal, who had a good knowledge of the local philosophy of astrology, realized a close correspondence between the calculations and observations of Caldecott with that of the native philosophy of astrology and proposed a thorough investigation of the correspondence between these two philosophies. Thus, Caldecott was appointed His Highness’s astronomer and the founding director of the Trivandrum Observatory (Fig. 1).[^2] The reason for an observatory at Trivandrum was that the institution was intended to give an impetus to science by taking advantage of the fact that the magnetic equator, the line on which the magnetic needle dips neither to north nor to south passes through the state of Travancore and the Maharaja desired that his country should partake with European nations in the scientific investigation (Horsley, 1838).
In the beginning, as there were no astronomical instruments, mere calculation work was done, and the results were published as *Astronomical Ephemeris adapted to the meridian of Trivandrum Observatory*. Caldecott started making observations in July 1837, using his own instrument (Caldecott, 1837b). Several portable instruments belonging to Caldecott were put into use, and a system of meteorological observations was also put in place at the same time.[^3] In the following years, he went to Europe to furnish the institution with the best instruments from Europe. During his later visit to London, Caldecott also purchased a complete set of magnetic and meteorological instruments. Caldecott returned to India in 1841, and the instruments he had brought were installed in their places during that year. A magnetic observatory was built in 1841. The instruments obtained by Caldecott for the magnetic observatory were of the same construction as those supplied to all observatories in the British dependencies, newly devised by Lloyd, and employed by him in the Dublin observatory. To facilitate the publication of the Almanac, a small printing establishment was started in 1836 (Caldecott, 1837b). The small printing press was subsequently enlarged. Printing machines were moved from England, and a printing department was opened. The first Government publication was the Anglo-Vernacular Calendar of Travancore for 1015 M.E (1839–1840). The printing establishment was also under the superintendence of Caldecott (1837a). A large mass of astronomical, magnetic, and meteorological observations was accumulated, and complete copies of them were forwarded to the Royal Society of London and the Court of Directors of the East India Company. These observations comprised the computed elements for the comets of 1843 and 1845 (Caldecott, 1845, 1846). In recognition of Caldecott’s works, he was elected a Fellow of the Royal Astronomical Society in 1840. He passed away at his house in Trivandrum on December 17, 1849.

In 1851, the Travancore state sent an invitation to John Alan Broun, a Fellow of the Royal Society, to take charge of the observatory. Broun took up the offer and joined in 1852 (Broun, 1857). Broun was interested in geomagnetic studies. He examined the laws of terrestrial magnetism and the variation of meteorological elements as influenced by height in the atmosphere. This, he thought, was possible only by simultaneous observations at two stations differing in height, as he felt that observations should not be limited to a single station but that the standard observatory should serve not merely for the determination of the laws and physical constants at one point, but also for the comparison and co-ordination of the laws depending on differences of height, of latitude and of longitude. The longitude and latitude of the site of the Trivandrum observatory were re-determined during his time, and the values arrived at were $76^{\circ} 59' 45''$ East longitude and $8^{\circ} 30' 32''$ North latitude of Greenwich (Broun, 1857). The height of the hillock on which the observatory was located was estimated to be 195 feet above sea level (Broun, 1873). A second observatory with a focus on magnetism and meteorology was established on the Agasthiyar mountain 6200 ft above sea level, as a result of the mammoth efforts of Broun (1873). The location of the observatory is given as latitude $8^{\circ} 38' \text{ N}$ and longitude as $5 \text{ h } 9 \text{ m } 15 \text{ s E}$ in the report submitted by Broun which needs a correction (Jayakrishnan, 2023a, 2023b). The second observatory commenced operation in May 1855 and continued to work up to 1859. Broun got infected because of the constant exposure to the cold winds and hot sun and had to leave for treatment in London in 1859. He returned after treatment in 1862 with permanent deafness in one of his ears. On his return, he once again got the observatory functional by 1864, and hourly observations were taken up to 1865. Hourly observations were stopped after 1865 citing the remoteness of the location and difficulties in maintaining the institution functional under the adverse weather conditions (Broun, 1873). In 1869, Broun retired from the directorship of the observatory with a lifetime pension from the Raja of Travancore. After leaving India, his whole time was devoted to the preparation of the first volume of the results of the Trivandrum observations (Broun, 1861, 1868). From London, where he resided for the last six years of his life, he published all of the works done by him at Trivandrum (Broun, 1872, 1873, 1877a, 1877b). For his various research works, he was awarded 1878 the Royal Medal of Royal Society of London. He returned to his home in Devonshire, England and passed away on 22 November 1879.[^4]

Dr. Alexander Crichton Mitchell, a professor of mathematics and physics at the His Highness The Maharajah’s College of Travancore was interested in meteorological sciences and volunteered to take charge of the observatory in 1890.[^5] Mitchell introduced a scheme of rainfall mapping for the princely state of Travancore by setting up a chain of meteorological stations located at Parur, Cheriyenkeezhu, Cherthala, Neyyatinkarra, Rajakamangalam, Tamarakulam, Ambalapuzha, Kartikapalli, Karunagapalli, Quilon and Vaviyur (Aiya, 1906a, 1906b). Under his directorship (1892–1910) the main activity of the observatory became signalling of time at 11:00 am to the Nair brigade, the official army of the Travancore government camped at the brigade ground. The observatory thus began to serve as a beacon for signaling time for the public during this period.[^6]
In 1911, Mr. J. Stephenson, Professor of Physics at the His Highness The Maharajah’s College of Travancore succeeded Mitchell as the Director (1911–1920) of the Observatory. He continued the work started by Mitchell till 1920. During the latter part of Mr. Stephenson’s time, he had an assistant director, Mr. M. Rama Varma Raja, who was a highly enthusiastic worker in the field of astronomy. For the years 1916 to 1919, Raja published an astronomical ephemeris. In 1920 Dr. K. R. Ramanathan succeeded Mr. Stephenson as Director (1920–1921) (Fig. 2). He conducted a study about thunderstorm activity in Trivandrum. In 1922, Dr. Ramanathan left Travancore service to join the Indian Meteorological Division, Government of India service.
Mr. Sivaramakrishna Iyer was confirmed as Honorary Director (1921–1926) when Ramanathan left the observatory.[^7] Self-recording instruments for meteorological measurements were set up in 1922, and the institution was recognized as a first class Meteorological Observatory by the India Meteorological Department. In 1927, the work of the observatory was divided into two sections: astronomical and meteorological, under the charge of the Government Astronomer Dr. H. Subramani Iyer (1927–1941) and the Government Meteorologist Mr. Sivaramakrishna Iyer then Director respectively. From that time, two independent sections started functioning in the observatory. With the creation of the Astronomical Department, a weekly publication of astronomical notes giving the position of the Sun, Moon, and the planets for Trivandrum and details of other important celestial phenomena were started. Other important works during that period include Celestial photography and from 1928 daily time signals being received by a wireless set[^6]. Till then, the mean time clock was standardized only by the star observations with the 4-inch transit.
In 1937, the Maharaja of Travancore Sri Chithirathirunal Balarama Varma established the University of Travancore. On August 17, 1939 the observatory was transferred to the control of the University, and it functioned as a unit of the Central Institute of Research.[^8] In the year 1940, the meteorological and astronomical sections amalgamated. The observatory attended primarily to the work of astronomical observations, calendar computation, and daily collection of data on rainfall. In 1951, a part of the work in the meteorological section was taken under the control of the India Meteorological Department.[^9] The astronomical section and a part of the meteorological work relating to rainfall registration remained under the state observatory. From January 18, 1960 the administrative control of the observatory was transferred to the Revenue Board, Trivandrum, and the observatory was ordered to function as an independent department under the charge of an Honorary Director.[^10] As there was a feeling that the development of the observatory was not getting proper attention and even its modest facilities were being underutilized, the Government of Kerala decided to transfer the observatory back to the control of the University. On January 1, 1976 the Government of Kerala transferred the observatory back to the control of the University of Kerala. Prof. Kalakad Sundaram Viswanathan, Nobel Laureate C. V. Raman’s student and the founding Professor and Head of the Teaching Department of Physics University of Kerala, was given the additional charge as the Honorary Director of the Observatory. This facilitated the department of physics and soon scientific production from which the department evolved in astrophysics (Nayar & Revathy, 1982; Revathy & Nayar, 1982). In 1988, Dr. S. R. Prabhakaran Nayar was given additional charge as the Honorary Director, and he revitalized the observatory by initiating an augmentation research project with funding from the Kerala State Council for Science, Technology and Environment. As the city lights polluted the sky, the observatory had a succession of directors who gradually moved the institution towards science popularization. In 2017, Dr. K. G. Gopchandran, the then director managed to procure two Schmidt–Cassegrain type telescopes one a 8″ and the other a 14″. The 8″ telescope was used for public outreach while the 14″ was made operational in 2022.

## 2 Heritage: observatory building

Lieutenant William Henry Horsley was given charge of constructing the observatory building in 1836 (Caldecott, 1837b). The site chosen was the tallest hill in Trivandrum, nearly 195 feet above sea level, commanding an unobstructed view of the sky on all sides. The position of the observatory was identified as with a Latitude of $8^{\circ} 30' 35''$ N and Longitude from Greenwich $76^{\circ} 59' 45''$ E. John Caldecott reported on the details of the observatory building constructed in Trivandrum through the *Madras Journal of Literature and Science* in 1837 (Caldecott, 1837a) (Fig. 3).
Horsley may have completed the building works by the middle of 1837 as the observations are reported to have started in July 1837 (Caldecott, 1837b)]. The original foundation stone laid on the building read as “THE TREVANDRUM OBSERVATORY” which may have been because of the name Trivandrum was given to the city by the British as a contraction of the original name and the colloquial use of the letter “e” for “i” may have been the notion then (Fig. 4).

The practical reason for the construction of the observatory opposite to the Kanakakunnu palace as this spot was topographically the most elevated location in the city. Caldecott reported that the sea was within two miles from the observatory and could be seen from the south to the west-northwest points of the horizon (Caldecott, 1837b). The observatory occupied the summit of a small hill in the heart of the city and was celebrated by travelers for the magnificent view that it commanded. The observatory had three domes on its terrace, which formed a nice mark of identification for the observatory, even from a great distance from the site. The mariners could observe these domes when they passed across Trivandrum on the Arabian Sea, and they formed a pleasing landmark for sailors on this coast for many years. In 1842, another building was erected to the north-west to mount the equatorial telescope constructed by Donald. The instrument occupied the central part of the building, and small rooms around it were used as the library.

The Agasthiyar observatory functioned till 27 February 1865, when the series of observations ceased, citing reasons like adverse climate, errors in instruments, transportation to and fro from the Agasthiyar peak, difficulty in meeting the expenses of the facility, and inadequate staff. The instruments were removed in March 1865. What happened to the building of the observing station at Agasthiyar mountain peak is not documented, and its remains also have not been retrieved.
There are no reports on any constructions or additions made to the building of the Trivandrum observatory after the tenure of Broun till 1931. In the centenary report of the observatory, there are photos taken during the directorship of Dr. A. C. Mitchell. The perspective of the building during that period, shown in Fig. 8, clearly shows the presence of two buildings that were present in the sketch of the observatory (Fig. 3). The architectural beauty of the three-dome structure of the building described by Broun is not evidenced in the image. This could be due to the direction from which the photo was taken.
In 1931, the observatory building was dismantled to make room for constructing a high-level water tank for distributing water in the town. The present structure shown in Fig. 10, was built in a spot adjacent to the reservoir. A vintage five-inch equatorial telescope from the early years of the observatory was shifted to the roof of the newly constructed water tank, where one can find it even today in working condition.

## 3 The priceless astronomical instruments

### 3.1 The sidereal clocks

The observatory possesses three sidereal clocks manufactured by Dent, imported from London in 1841, and considered to be of the best construction during that period. The pendulum bracket for these clocks was fixed to the granite pier of the observatory (Caldecott, 1837b), where they can also be found today (Fig. 13). As proof of these clocks’ quality, one is functional even to this day (Narayanan, 2020).

### 3.2 The mural circle

The mural circle by Troughton and Simms was placed on the east side of the observatory. This was of the same construction as that used at the Greenwich Observatory. ### 3.3 The equatorial

In 1842, to the north-west of the building, an equatorial telescope, the make of which was Dollard, was installed. The telescope was a 5-inch (12.7 cm) refractor with a $f/16.8$ focal length. ## 4 Astronomical sightings

Caldecott’s report on the observation of the great comet of 1843 from Trivandrum was published in the year 1846 in *Memoirs of the Royal Astronomical Society* (Caldecott, 1846). The comet was first observed partially on 4th March, and its tail was seen on 5th March. The next day, there was a lot of public excitement, and the observations could not be conducted accurately. He calculated and presented the elements of the orbit of the comet in this communication based on his measurements made on the 8th, 13th, and 18th March 1843. He had used an achromatic telescope of 90″ focal length and 5″ aperture made by Dollond for his measurements. Caldecott noted that the tail had a dark appearance along its axis, and about halfway from the head, the tail appeared to separate into two, with the upper being longer than the lower part. Caldecott documented that the comet was not visible from 6th April 1843.
The observation of the comet 1844–1845 was first made on 30th December 1844. Based on his observation of the comet on 9th, 14th, and 19th January 1945 the parabolic elements of the orbit were calculated and published without correcting for the parallax and aberrations (Caldecott, 1845). He reported that the comet initially presented a distinct nucleus resembling a fifth-magnitude star that faded subsequently. To pinpoint the comet’s location, he mentioned that a star of magnitude 7 preceding the comet by $18^s.2$ was visible along with the comet. The comet could be seen using a telescope up to 6th February 1845.
Nearly a century later, Dr. H. Subramania Iyer sighted a new comet 1941-C at the observatory on the morning of 23rd January 1941 and communicated it to Greenwich. The original reports by Dr. Subramania were not published and are not traceable today. It is reported that by the time his cable reached Greenwich, other observatories around the world had already reported the sighting. For his 23rd January observation of the comet, Subramania Iyer was also an independent discoverer alongside J. S. Paraskevopoulos and R. Grandon (Shylaja, 2022). The comet was kept under observation for a month using the 5″ telescope, and the orbit of the comet was computed by P. K. Kuttan Nair who was working as an assistant at the observatory (Kapoor, 2013). He later became a mathematics professor at the University College and was given additional charge as the observatory director before retiring from service (Table 2).
The opposition of Jupiter by its satellite Io on 26th September 2022 was recorded as the observatory’s first light after installation of the 14″ SCT (Jayakrishnan, 2023a). In 2023, Jayakrishnan published a report on tracking the comet C/2023 E3(ZTF) from the observatory. The comet could be tracked only on the 13th, 14th, 15th, and 16th February 2023 because of the poor weather conditions (Jayakrishnan, 2023b).

## 6 Conclusions

The Trivandrum Observatory was very different than the observatories run by the East India Company at Bombay (Colaba) (established 1826) and Madras (established 1786), both of which were military establishments based within forts, while the Trivandrum Observatory was an institution envisioned for the educational and cultural growth of the state by its princely rulers. Through John Caldecott’s efforts, the observatory established itself among the best equipped observatories in India during the 1840s. Caldecott laid the foundations for the meteorological studies, and the successive observatory directors moved it away from astronomy. A century after Caldecott’s trysts with astronomy from the observatory, works by Dr. Subramania brought back astronomy as the mainstay of the institution in the 1940s. However, successive changes in the ownership of the institution did not help build on the vision of the observatory’s founders. Despite having such a rich legacy, the observatory has not been able to become a beacon of research in astronomy. Today, the institution houses many antique-valued working telescopes and mechanical clocks, which are among the oldest of their kind in India. In 2023, a scientific production from the Astronomical Observatory was published, pointing out the lost Agasthiyar observatory and its contributions to the global scientific community. The report itself was revealing as the local scientific community had forgotten the Agasthiyar observatory. The Trivandrum observatory will complete 200 years of existence in a few more years. The onus is on the University’s research faculty to modernize the astronomical observatory by introducing state-of-the-art optics and computational tools so that the institution can become a thought leader in Astronomy for which the Travancore King originally envisioned it. One would have to agree that Trivandrum Observatory has not been able to evolve in the last two centuries to be recognized as a major international player in astronomy. This article summarizes the astronomical contributions from the observatory to revitalize the ongoing efforts to modernize the observatory and bring back the bygone academic grandeur.
