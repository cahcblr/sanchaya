### Abstract

The conceptualisation of negative numbers represents a fascinating aspect of mathematical development, characterised by its evolution over time and its multifaceted applications across various disciplines. This paper embarks on a scholarly exploration of this intriguing subject, focusing on a salient segment from Kṛṣṇa Daivajña’s *Bījapallava*, a detailed commentary of Bhāskarācārya’s *Bījagaṇita*. It situates the discussion within its historical and scholarly context by examining the complexities inherent in understanding ‘negative’ quantities. While briefly referencing the evolution of negative numbers by notable mathematicians such as Brahmagupta and Bhāskara, the primary focus remains on dissecting a section of the “*dhanarṇaṣaḍvidha*” excerpt from *Bījapallava*, thereby shedding light on significant aspects of the discussion on negative numbers.
**Keywords** *dhana* · *ṛṇa* · *dhanarṇaṣaḍvidha* · *Bījapallava* · *Kṛṣṇa Daivajña* · Negative numbers · Indian mathematics

## 1 Introduction

The concept of numbers is one of the most ancient, important, and fascinating inventions of the human mind. The representation of numbers using different systems across various civilisations over the ages is an amusing and engaging story in itself.[^1] Typically, the concept of numbers is introduced to a child by using physical objects or their sketches on paper. Therefore, we generally understand a number to denote the count or measure of something. Consequently, the notion of negative numbers and the notion of negativity inherent in numbers would have been intriguing to people for centuries.
In the textbooks on mathematics, negative numbers are generally denoted by positioning them to the left of zero on a numerical line. Despite the commonplace usage of phrases such as “negative thinking,” the precise connotation of negativity as an attribute of or adjective for numbers may not be immediately apparent to those encountering it for the first time. Upon observing the number line, one may intuit that negative numbers denote quantities lesser than zero. However, a more thorough investigation is necessary to fully ascertain their conceptual significance.
This paper aims to explore the conceptualisation of negative numbers by delving into a salient textual excerpt from Kṛṣṇa Daivajña’s *Bījapallava*, focusing on the conceptualisation of negative numbers. To provide a comprehensive understanding, it situates this investigation within a broader historical and scholarly context, emphasising the complexities inherent in grappling with the concept of negatives. Drawing on a rich lineage of mathematical thinkers including Brahmagupta and Bhāskara, the paper traces the trajectory of negative numbers in Indian mathematics. It discusses how negative numbers were applied in diverse fields such as accounting, algebraic expressions related to spherical trigonometry, and astronomy. Given that *Bījapallava* is a prominent commentary of *Bījagaṇita*, the paper also examines the significance of commentarial traditions in shaping mathematical discourse in ancient India. By closely scrutinising a specific excerpt from the “*dhanarṇaṣaḍvidha*” of *Bījapallava*, the paper aims to illuminate how the complex concept of negative numbers was elegantly introduced, highlighting the practical applications associated within the Indian mathematical tradition.

## 2 Precursor to the notion of negative quantities

The terms that are generally used for the positive (+ve) and negative (−ve) quantities in the Indian mathematical treatises are *dhana* (wealth) and *ṛṇa* (debt) respectively. One of the earliest references for the use of the word *ṛṇa* can be traced back to the Vedic corpus. We have the following passage in the *Kṛṣṇa-yajurveda*:
> जायमानो वै ब्राह्मणस्त्रिभिरृणवा जायते ब्रह्मचर्येण ऋषिभ्यो यज्ञेन देवेभ्यः प्रजया पितृभ्य एष वा अनृणो यः पुत्री यज्वा ब्रह्मचारिवासी तदवदानैरेवाव दयते तदवदानानामवदानत्वम् । (*तै.सं.* ६.३.१०.५)
>
> *jāyamāno vai brāhmaṇastribhirṛṇavā jāyate brahmacaryeṇa ṛṣibhyo yajñena devebhyaḥ prajayā pitṛbhya eṣa vā anṛṇo yaḥ puttrī yajvā brahmacārivāsī tadavadānairevāva dayate tadavadānānām avadānatvam |*
> (*Taittirīyasaṃhitā 6.3.10.5*)
The above passage essentially states that an individual is born with three debts, namely:
1. the debt towards the seers and scholars of the past, whose wisdom has provided us with a vision to shape our lives and build a harmonious atmosphere around us,
2. the debt to the nature around us (personified as divine forces), whose blessings have provided us with a conducive environment to live, and finally
3. the debt towards the ancestors in our family, to whom we completely owe our existence today.
This passage was cited primarily to draw readers’ attention to the use of the the word ‘*ṛṇa*’ in one of the most ancient literatures that has survived intact to date. Since further discussion on this topic is not germane to this paper, without much elaboration, we will proceed to highlight other ancient texts that discuss negative quantities.

## 3 Brahmagupta’s rule for dealing with negatives

By the 7th century CE, one of the most prominent Indian mathematicians, Brahmagupta, discussed the application of negative numbers in detail. It has been generally accepted among the historians of mathematics that he was the first to provide a detailed explanation of operations involving both positive and negative numbers. His contributions include the conceptualisation of zero as the result of subtracting a number from itself and the formulation of rules governing arithmetic operations on negative and positive numbers, as well as surds. Negative numbers feature prominently in Brahmagupta’s treatise, *Brāhmasphuṭasiddhānta* (628 CE). The following verses from the 18th chapter illustrate the arithmetic of zero, positive and negative numbers and it is noteworthy that, with the sole exception of zero divided by zero being zero, these are identical to their counterparts in modern algebra:[^2]
> धनयोर्धनमृणमृणयोर्धनर्णयोरन्तरं समैक्यं खम् ।
> ऋणमैक्यं च धनमृणधनशून्ययोः शून्ययोः शून्यम् ॥ १८.३० ॥
> शून्यविहीनमृणमृणं धनं धनं भवति शून्यमाकाशम् ।
> शोध्यं यदा धनमृणादृणं धनाद्वा तदा क्षेप्यम् ॥ १८.३२ ॥
> ऋणमृणधनयोर्घातो धनमृणयोधनवधो धनं भवति ।
> शून्यर्णयोः खधनयोः खशून्ययोर्वा वधः शून्यम् ॥ १८.३३ ॥
> धनभक्तं धनमृणहृतमृणं धनं भवति खभक्तं खम् ।
> भक्तमृणेन धनमृणं धनेन हृतमृणं मृणं भवति ॥ १८.३४ ॥
> खोध्दृतमृणं धनं वा तच्छेदं खमृणधनविभक्तं वा ।
> ऋणधनयोर्वर्गः स्वं खं खस्य पदं कृतिर्यत्तत् ॥ १८.३५ ॥
>
> *dhanayor dhanam ṛṇam ṛṇayor dhanarṇayor antaraṃ samaikyaṃ kham |*
> *ṛṇam aikyaṃ ca dhanam ṛṇadha naśūnyayoḥ śūnyayoḥ śūnyam ||18.30||*
> *śūnyavihīnam ṛṇam ṛṇaṃ dhanaṃ dhanaṃ bhavati śūnyam ākāśam |*
> *śodhyaṃ yadā dhanam ṛṇād ṛṇam dhanād vā tadā kṣepyam ||18.32||*
> *ṛṇam ṛṇadhanayor ghāto dhanam ṛṇayor dhanavadho dhanaṃ bhavati |*
> *śūnyarṇayoḥ khadhanayoḥ khaśūnyayor vā vadhaḥ śūnyam ||18.33||*
> *dhanabhaktaṃ dhanam ṛṇahṛtam ṛṇam dhanaṃ bhavati khabhaktaṃ kham |*
> *bhaktam ṛṇena dhanam ṛṇaṃ dhanena hṛtam ṛṇam ṛṇaṃ bhavati ||18.34||*
> *khoddhṛtam ṛṇaṃ dhanaṃ vā tacchedaṃ kham ṛṇadhanavibhaktaṃ vā |*
> *ṛṇadhanayor vargaḥ svaṃ khaṃ khasya padaṃ kṛtir yat tat ||18.35||*
* ... [The sum of] positive (*dhana*) and negative (*ṛṇa*), if they are equal, is zero (*kha*). The sum of a negative and zero is negative, of a positive and zero is positive and of two zeros, zero (*śūnya*).
* ... Zero subtracted from negative is negative, from positive is positive, and from zero is zero (*ākāśa*).
* ... The product of zero and a negative, of zero and a positive, or of two zeroes is zero.
* ... A zero divided by zero is zero. ... A positive or a negative divided by zero is that with zero-denominator.[^3]
The treatise presents precise guidelines for the arithmetic of negative numbers, with positive and negative integers denoted by the terms *dhana* and *ṛṇa*: literally meaning “wealth/ fortune” and “debt” respectively. It is, therefore, evident that the practice of accounting is highly likely to have facilitated the natural integration of negative numbers into arithmetic computations.

## 4 Compelling contexts for the evaluation of negatives

The conjecture that it was the highly developed tradition of accounting in India which led to a fuller understanding of negative numbers has some scholarly endorsement from Mattessich.[^4] However, David Mumford, in his eminently readable historiography entitled “What’s So Baffling About Negative Numbers?” muses as to “what considerations led Indian mathematicians in the late centuries BCE or the early centuries CE to these conclusions—especially for the multiplication of negative numbers” and points out how the introduction of negative numbers paved the path for deeper explorations in algebra:[^5]
> Perhaps the deepest of these was the Indian work on Pell’s equation $x^2 - Ny^2 = m$ — especially finding solutions for $m = 1$: Brahmagupta himself made the first huge step, discovering the multiplication law arising from the factorization
> $$x^2 - Ny^2 = (x + \sqrt{N}y)(x - \sqrt{N}y).$$

> More exactly, he showed how from solutions of the equation for $m_1, m_2$, one gets one for their product $m = m_1 \dots m_2$.
Additionally, early contributions to Indian mathematics were closely interwoven with advancements in astronomy, calendrics, and time reckoning. Mathematical inquiry, as well as the development of mathematical concepts and techniques, served, at least initially, as an auxiliary discipline to facilitate and aid the practical needs of understanding celestial phenomena and maintaining accurate time measurements.
The conceptualisation of the negatives was also applied in Indian astronomy.[^6] In the tradition of astronomical scholarship of specifying variables pertaining to astronomical measurements, negative numbers find yet another application in the realm of coordinates, particularly in measuring celestial latitude (separation along a great circle, perpendicular to the ecliptic) or declination (separation along a great circle, perpendicular to the celestial equator) of celestial objects such as planets and stars.
The conception of these physical quantities measured in opposite directions as positive and negative becomes evident from the exposition provided by Kṛṣṇa Daivajña (see Sect. 7.4.2). Despite the natural fit for negative numbers in these contexts, tradition often dictates the use of north/south designations rather than positive/negative, posing a challenge to altering this convention. However, in the context of converting a pair of values specifying the position of the planets in one coordinate system to the other and when calculating the rising times of the Sun, etc., positives and negatives frequently need to be incorporated into formulas. They are also employed in the calculation of the duration of *lagnas*, as well as in determining sine values themselves. Thus, we find compelling contexts where rules for negatives are used.

## 5 Commentatorial traditions in Indic scholarship

Usually, the source texts in the Indian tradition, particularly in ancient times, have been composed adopting a terse style of versification. This is true irrespective of the discipline—be it philosophy, aesthetics, astronomy or mathematics. Consequently, in order to fully appreciate the content of these texts, we need to resort to commentaries authored at much later times, sometimes even a millenia later. Within Indic scholarship, commentaries serve a significant function not only in understanding the content of the text, but also in appreciating the epistemic structure of knowledge systems. Consistent with the customary practice, we encounter multiple commentaries on a single text, be it in philosophy, literature, music, astronomy or mathematics. For instance, *Līlāvatī* of Bhāskara II, undoubtedly one of the most elegant and foundational texts in the genre, has several extant commentaries, such as *Buddhivilāsinī*, *Kriyākramakarī*, *Līlāvatīvivaraṇa*, *Līlāvatīvāsanā*, *Gaṇitāmṛtalaharī* and so on. Likewise, his *Bījagaṇita* garnered considerable scholarly attention in the commentatorial tradition. This prominence prompted numerous scholars to produce commentaries on the text, including *Bālabodhinī* (also known as *Bījodāharaṇa*, *Bījagaṇitavivṛti*, or *Bījagaṇitavyākhyā*), *Bījapallava* (alternatively titled *Bījavivṛti-kalpalatāvatāra* or *Kalpalatāvatāra*), *Bījavivṛtikalpalatā*, *Bījaprabodha*, *Bījagaṇitatīkā*, and *Sūryaprakāśabījavyākhyā*.[^7] Of these, Kṛṣṇa Daivajña’s *Bījapallava* seems to have been the most popular one.

## 6 Kṛṣṇa Daivajña’s *Bījapallava*

Kṛṣṇa Daivajña’s *Bījapallava* is an extensive commentary on Bhāskarācārya’s *Bījagaṇita*, written during the late sixteenth century. We can comprehend how elaborate this commentary is from a succinct note provided by the author himself. At the end of the commentary, after presenting a brief account of his academic lineage—just as Bhāskara himself does—Kṛṣṇa Daivajña notes that his commentary altogether has about 4500 *granthas*:
> एवमादितो ग्रन्थसंख्या ४४५८ ।
> *evamādito granthasaṃkhyā 4458 |*
> Thus, the number of the *granthas* from the beginning is 4458.
Though the word *grantha* has various connotations, in this context, it means 32 syllables, which is essentially what a verse composed in *anuṣṭubh* metre has. Bhāskara at the end of *Bījagaṇita* notes:
> अत्रानुष्टुप् सहस्रं हि ससूत्रोद्देश्यके मितिः ।
> *atrānuṣṭupsahasraṃ hi sasūtroddeśyake mitiḥ |*
> In this (book), including *sūtra* and *uddeśya*, the measure is one thousand *anuṣṭups* [32000 syllables].
Thus we see that the commentary, *Bījapallava*, is approximately 4.5 times larger in size than the text *Bījagaṇita*, along with his own *vāsanā* by Bhāskara.[^8]

### 6.1 Lineage of Kṛṣṇa Daivajña and his work

Kṛṣṇa Daivajña, a distinguished scholar of his era, belonged to a venerable lineage of astronomers and mathematicians. His academic and genealogical lineage, detailed at the end of his work *Bījapallava*, reveals a rich heritage of scholarly excellence and devotion. His ancestor Śrīkeśava, a master of the discipline of *gaṇita*, passed down his extensive knowledge to Gaṇeśa, whose virtuous fame and mastery of *jyotiṣaśāstra* were highly celebrated. Gaṇeśa’s nephew, Nṛsiṃha, carried this tradition of excellence forward, and his disciple Viṣṇu further advanced their scholarly lineage. It was under Viṣṇu’s guidance that Kṛṣṇa Daivajña studied all the three branches of *jyotiṣa* and emerged as a preeminent scholar. Fig. 1 provides the academic lineage of Kṛṣṇa Daivajña over three generations.
Kṛṣṇa Daivajña’s familial lineage, delineated in Fig. 2, is equally illustrious. His ancestor Cintāmaṇi, known for his devotion and virtues, was revered to the extent that Goddess Gaurī herself is said to have appeared in response to his prayers. Cintāmaṇi’s lineage included Rāma, a highly esteemed figure whose foresight was sought by the King of Vidarbha. Rāma’s sons, Trimalla and Gopirāja, and subsequently Trimalla’s son Ballāla, continued to enhance the family’s scholarly reputation. Ballāla’s son, Kṛṣṇa Daivajña, maintained and elevated this legacy through his many notable contributions.
Kṛṣṇa Daivajña produced the *Chādakanirṇaya*, an original piece on eclipse calculation, and several other scholarly commentaries, including those on Bhāskara’s *Līlāvatī* and Śrīpati’s *Jātakapaddhati*. Furthermore, he is said to have played a pivotal role among the cohort of scholars at the court tasked with the Sanskrit translation of Ulugh Beg’s astronomical tables.

> तत्कृष्णभूमिमधिगम्य विचारवारि-
> संसिक्तमाङ्कुरजनुष्यभवत् समर्थम् ॥ ६ ॥
>
> *yadbhāskareṇa nijadhāmaguṇātirekāt*

## 7 Conceptualisation of negative numbers: textual analysis of the excerpt on *dhanarṇaṣaḍvidha* from *Bījapallava*

The excerpt “*dhanarṇaṣaḍvidha*” of *Bījapallava* follows a meticulously structured approach, systematically introducing and expanding upon mathematical concepts in a logical sequence. As noted earlier, the terms *dhana* and *ṛṇa* refer to wealth (possession) and debt as well as positive and negative.

### 7.1 Introducing positive and negative through commonplace example

The section under consideration, a prose piece, commences with the sentence:
एवं देवदत्तस्य मुद्रात्रयमेकं धनम् । अन्यदपि मुद्राचतुष्टयं धनमस्तीत्युक्ते अस्त्यस्य मुद्रासप्तकं धनमिति विलसति सार्वजनीनो व्यवहारः ।
*evaṃ devadattasya mudrātrayamekaṃ dhanam | anyadapi mudrācatuṣṭayaṃ dhanamastītyukte astyasya mudrāsaptakaṃ dhanamiti vilasati sārvajanīno vyavahāraḥ |*
Thus, Devadatta has three *mudrās*. If you say that he has another four *mudrās*, then that he has [totally] seven *mudrās* in his possession is a common discourse among all people.
The fundamental mathematical concept of addition is mentioned at the outset. The commentator uses an illustration featuring a character named Devadatta. Described as possessing three *mudrās* or coins[^10] initially, Devadatta is said to further have four additional *mudrās* [received as a gift]. It is then of common knowledge (*sārvajanīno vyavahāraḥ*) that Devadatta is now in possession of a total of seven *mudrās*.
Taking the discussion further ahead, the commentator states that if Devadatta has three *mudrās* of wealth in his possession, and a debt or *ṛṇa* of four *mudrās*, then, again, the prevalent notion among all (*sakalajanasādhāraṇo vyavahāraḥ*) would be that he has a debt of one *mudrā*. Likewise, if he has a debt of three *mudrās* and has four *mudrās* with him, then he would be thought of as being debt-free and having a *mudrā* left with him. Here, the commentator is, in essence, evoking, illustrating and expatiating upon two quotes from *Bījagaṇita* that he employs to serve as the foundation for his subsequent conceptualisations and discussions:
योगे युतिः स्यात् क्षययोः स्वर्वा धनर्णयोरन्तरमेव योगः ।
*yoge yutiḥ syāt kṣayayoḥ svayorvā dhanarṇayorantarameva yogaḥ |*
When the two numbers are both positive (*dhana*) or negative (*ṛṇa*) then the sum (*yoga*) is obtained by addition. [If not] the difference of the positive and negative is the sum.
It is well known in the world, that if the positive (wealth in one’s own possession) and negative (debt) are of equal magnitudes, they nullify each other leading to zero as net possession. On the other hand if they are of different magnitudes, then whichever is of greater magnitude is what remains positive or negative as the net possession. The basic idea is that two debts can be combined into a single debt of greater magnitude. This narrative introduction sets the stage for further explanation and exploring mathematical operations within a relatable framework.

### 7.2 The fundamentality of addition

Following this introduction, the commentator goes on to mention, in passing, the fundamental arithmetic operations of addition and subtraction (*saṃkalana* and *vyavakalana*) of both fractions and whole numbers (*bhinnānām abhinnānāṃ ca*). He also justifies the inclusion and omission of certain operations for whole numbers or integers (*rūpavarṇa*) vis-à-vis surds (*karaṇī*). Then he notes that it is apt to introduce addition before other operations such as subtraction, as they are all dependent on addition (*saṃkalanopajīvakatva*). Subtraction as well as multiplication are based on addition. Here subtraction is visualised as the addition of a negative value:
तथापि धनर्णताव्यत्यासमात्रविलक्षणस्य व्यवकलनस्य
*tathāpi dhanarṇatāvyatyāsamātravilakṣaṇasya*
Even then, it [the subtraction] is different [from the addition] only because of the nature of the quantities being positive and negative.
Addition with a negative quantity gives the difference. When both quantities are negative, they are added using the mathematical operator of addition. The following verse from *Bījagaṇita* is quoted in this context to bring greater clarity:
संशोध्यमानं स्वमृणत्वमेति स्वत्वं क्षयस्तद्युतिरुक्तवच्च ।
*saṃśodhyamānaṃ svamṛṇatvameti svatvaṃ kṣaya-stadyutiruktavacca |*
A positive [number], that is being subtracted (*saṃśodhyamānaṃ*), becomes a negative [number],[^11] and, a negative [number] (*kṣayaḥ*) [becomes] positive. And their addition is as stated earlier.
In providing the translation to the above verse, it may be noted that we have supplied that verb *eti* which means ‘becomes’ again. It is quite common in Sanskrit tradition to be highly conservative and unostentatious with respect to the use of words. That we are right, and have not taken any additional liberty in doing so, is evident from the commentary. It is said:
तद्यदि धनमस्ति तर्हि ऋणत्वमेति । यदि क्षयोऽस्ति तर्हि धनत्वमेति ।
*tadyadi dhanamasti tarhi rṇatvameti | yadi kṣayo'sti tarhi dhanatvameti |*
If it (i.e., the quantity to be subtracted) is positive, then it (positive) becomes negative. If it is negative (*kṣaya*), it becomes positive.
Having succinctly summarised how to deal with the basic mathematical operations, the commentary proceeds to very systematically introduce the concept of negative quantities.

### 7.3 Do negative numbers really exist?

It was quite natural for Kṛṣṇa Daivajña to commence the discussion with positive quantity and first establish the fundamental and straightforward nature of the addition rule (*saṃkalana*), before elaborating extensively on subtraction (*vyavakalana*). This is because even in real-world scenarios such as borrowing money, the borrowed amount is, after all, a positive quantity. These descriptions may prompt the readers to embark upon thinking that negative quantities exist solely at a conceptual level; there are no real negative numbers. Whatever needs to be removed from one’s possession or deducted is conceived as negative. Again, when two quantities, both of the same nature positive or negative (*kṣayayoḥ va svayorvā*), need to be added, the operation is addition. So, shall we say that the negative numbers are mere mental constructs?
From a certain viewpoint, yes we can say so and this can be appreciated too. However, negative numbers are as fundamental as positive numbers in mathematics! If negative numbers are mental constructs so too are positive numbers!
In mathematical computations, we require negative notations to accurately represent situations such as debt or deficits. More often than not, it is such real-life needs, applications and observations that inspire specific modes of mathematical reasoning and abstraction, ultimately leading to the conceptualisation and creation of abstract yet highly useful entities such as negative numbers.

### 7.4 Categorisation of negative entities: spatial, temporal and possessional

In the ensuing portion, Kṛṣṇa Daivajña proposes a lucid categorisation of the concept of negative entities into three distinct types:
ऋणत्वमिह त्रिधा तावदस्ति - देशतः, कालतः, वस्तुतश्चेति ।
*ṛṇatvamiha tridhā tāvadasti - deśataḥ, kālataḥ, vastutaśceti |*
Negativeness is indeed of three types — pertaining to location (or space), time, and possession (or ownership) [of objects].
He also defines the negative [in each of these categories] as oppositeness or contrariety: *tacca vaiparītyameva*. The negative is thus characterised by polarity, antithesis, or possessing an opposite character. Furthermore, it is noteworthy that the passage extends its exploration beyond basic arithmetic, delving into broader applications of negative values.
The concept of the negative with respect to space is explained using three examples.

#### 7.4.1 Geometric demonstration of the negative in terms of directionality

The first demonstration or *upapatti* uses a geometric construction described in the text *Līlāvatī*. The case in point is verse 168, appearing in the chapter on geometry titled “*Kṣetravyavahāra*”. This verse is presented by Bhāskara as a problem to be solved in connection with finding the alititude (*lambaka*) of a triangle. This specific problem is entitled *ṛṇābādhodāharaṇa* which means an example when the *ābādhā* [^12] is negative:
दशसप्तदशप्रमौ भुजौ त्रिभुजे यत्र नवप्रमा मही ।
अबाधे वद लम्बकं तथा गणितं गाणितिकाशु तत्र मे ॥ १६८ ॥
*daśasaptadaśapramau bhujau tribhuje yatra navapramā mahī |*
*abadhe vada lambakaṃ tathā gaṇitaṃ gāṇitikāśu tatra me || 168 ||*
In a triangle which has sides of measure 10 and seventeen and the base is measured nine, O mathematician, tell me immediately the altitude (*lambaka*), the two segments (*abadhe*) of the base and the area (*gaṇita*).
The problem posed by Bhāskara can be understood with the help of Fig. 3. Here $ABC$ is the triangle whose two sides and the base are specified in the verse to be 10, 17 and 9 respectively. Now the problem is to find the altitude $BD$. It is evident that the triangle $ABC$ is an obtuse triangle. Hence, Bhāskara demonstrates how to find the solution to the problem. He indicates that the two *ābādhās*, $AD$ and $CD$, are 15 and 6 respectively. The foot of the perpendicular, $BD$, falls outside the base $AC$ of the obtuse triangle $ABC$, forming a negative *ābādhā*, $CD$. Of this excellent geometric demonstration of the conceptualisation of negative distance, Mumford remarks as follows:[^13]
> This is stated so casually, as if it were common wisdom, that one can only conclude that this way of thinking about negative distances was well-known in his time. Nonetheless, as we will see, it doesn’t occur in Europe before the work of Wallis near the end of 17th century.
Kṛṣṇa Daivajña observes that, in terms of directionality, the *ṛṇābādhā* has the nature of reversal or *viparītasya bhāva* as is required by negative entities:
ऋणगता आबाधा दिग्‍वैपरीत्येन इत्यर्थ इति ।
*ṛṇagatā ābādhā digvaiparītyena ityartha iti |*
The *ābādhā* on the negative side is because of the opposition in direction.

#### 7.4.2 The negative visualised with the aid of number line and directions

In the earlier geometric example, the concept of the negative in the context of directionality assumes that the two segments lying on either side of $C$ are designated as positive and negative. This suggests that the measurements are positive in one direction and negative in the other. This idea is neatly elucidated by Kṛṣṇa Daivajña as follows:
तत्र एका रेखा स्थिता । द्वितीया दिक् विपरीता दिक् इत्युच्यते, यथा पूर्वविपरीता पश्चिमा दिक्, यथा वा उत्तरदिग्विपरीता दक्षिणा दिक् इत्यादि । तथा च पूर्वापरदेशयोर्मध्ये एकतरस्य धनत्वे कल्पिते तं प्रति तदितरस्य ऋणत्वम् ।
*tatra ekā rekhā sthitā | dvitīyā dik viparītā dik ityucyate, yathā pūrvaviparītā paścimā dik, yathā vā uttaradigviparītā dakṣiṇā dik ityādi | tathā ca pūrvāparadeśayormadhye ekatarasya dhanatve kalpite taṃ prati taditarasya ṛṇatvam |*
There is [essentially] one line. [Having marked a point in that line], the other direction is said to be the opposite direction, much like how east is opposite to west, or south is opposite to the north and so on. Thus among the eastern and the western regions, if one is designated as positive, then the other is considered negative.
Here, *ṛṇatva* (negativeness) is introduced by invoking the concept of displacement along the opposite (*viparīta*) directions in a line, by considering segments along the line in opposite direction, having chosen a point of reference in that line as ‘origin’. This, dating back to circa 1150 AD, could be deemed as “the first occurrence of the “number line”, of using positive and negative numbers as coordinates on either side of an origin.”[^14] Having discussed the notion of negativity in connection with the displacement along a line in the opposite directions, the commentator next proceeds to conceptualise the negative using observational astronomy (Fig. 4).

#### 7.4.3 Use of negatives in connection with the motion of celestial objects

The phenomenon of apparent retrogradation among planetary bodies in the solar system has long intrigued ancient astronomers. All the celestial bodies, the Sun, Moon, planets and stars have a certain uniform motion westwards.[^15] This motion (from east to west) is purely apparent and is due to the rotation of the Earth. However, the Sun, Moon and planets have their proper motion towards the east, and this causes their longitudes to increase. Of course, the rate at which the longitude increases is different for different celestial bodies.
For the Sun and the Moon, the longitudes keep increasing continuously. On an average, the rate at which the Moon’s longitude is $\approx 13^\circ/\text{day}$ whereas for the Sun it is close to one degree per day. In the case of planets, as they keep traversing eastwards, something bizarre happens. During their orbital motion, at certain points in their orbits, the planets appear to take a momentary halt from their eastward drift. And in the subsequent days, it would appear as if they are moving westwards having taken a reverse course in their motion. This phenomenon could continue for a few days, a few weeks, or a few months (as in the case of Saturn) depending upon the planet. After this, once again they may halt momentarily and continue their eastward motion.
In this manner, all the five planets, Mercury, Venus, Mars, Jupiter and Saturn—that are visible to the naked eyes—seem to exhibit this kind of ‘strange’ motion called retrograde in English and *vakragati* in Sanskrit, of the planets noted by the observers on the Earth. This apparent reversal in direction of motion has to do with the relative positions of the planet, the Earth, and the Sun. Kṛṣṇa Daivajña invokes this particular planetary phenomenon called *viparītasya bhāva* to illustrate *dhanaṛṇatva*, the notion of positive and negative:
पूर्वगतेर्धनत्वकल्पने यदा ग्रहः पश्चिमगतिर्भवति तदा ग्रहे गतितुल्यकला ऋणं भवति । यथा वा पश्चिमभ्रमस्य धनत्वे यावद् ग्रहः पूर्वतो गच्छति तावत् पश्चिमभ्रमे ऋणमिति । दक्षिणोत्तरदेशादिष्वप्येवमेव धनर्णत्वं बोध्यम् ।
*pūrvagaterdhanatvakalpane yadā grahaḥ paścimagatirbhavati tadā grahe gatitulyakalā ṛṇaṃbhavati | yathā vā paścimabhramasya dhanatve yāvad grahaḥ pūrvato gacchati tāvat paścimabhrame ṛṇamiti | dakṣiṇottaradeśādiṣvapyevameva dhanaṛṇatvaṃ bodhyam |*
If movement towards the east is considered positive, when a planet moves towards the west, then that amount of motion in minutes (*gatitulyakalā*) is considered negative. Conversely, if the westward movement is regarded as positive, then as much as the planet moves eastwards, that much (*tāvat*) is conceived to be negative (*ṛṇam*) in [terms of] westward motion (*paścimabhrame*). It may be understood that this way of conceiving positive and negative is applicable to the regions in the north-south directions and so on.[^16]
Here the commentator brilliantly makes use of the commonly observed phenomenon of apparent retrograde motion of planets to explain the concept of negativity. This choice could be based on two reasons:
*   to facilitate the understanding that the system of positive and negative is not confined to describing things in the terrestrial frame, but also in the celestial frame.
*   to indicate that what matters is the directionality that is invoked by us in describing the phenomenon and not whether the direction is conceived on the ground or in the space.
Given that astronomy or *jyotiṣa* is essentially the science of time reckoning (*kālavidhāna śāstra*), it is fairly intuitive that the text next addresses time as a means to conceive of the notion of the negative.

### 7.5 The concept of the negative in temporal terms

Having introduced the concept of -ve numbers using spatial coordinates, Kṛṣṇa Daivajña moves on to explain the same by making use of the concept of travelling back in time. The negativity of earlier and later times may be exemplified in determining the day as well as through the organisation of the days of the week, where each day follows a sequential order from the beginning (Sunday) to the end (Saturday):
एवं पूर्वोत्तरकालयोः अन्योन्यं ऋणत्वं वारप्रवृत्त्यादिषु प्रसिद्धम् ।
*evaṃ pūrvottarakālayoḥ anyonyam ṛṇatvaṃ vārapravṛttyādiṣu prasiddham |*
Similarly, the relativity (*anyonya*) of the negativity of the previous and later times (*pūrvottara kāla*) is well known with reference to the passage of the day and so on.
We know that the past was indeed present and the future is also going to be present. Thus, Kṛṣṇa Daivajña, by presenting this example, buttresses the notion of negativity by helping us think of the concept in multiple ways.

### 7.6 The concept of the negative in ownership

The concept of negative quantities is further elucidated in the context of ownership or possession. Here, the *vaiparītya* is demonstrated concerning the relationship of ownership (*svasvāmibhāva-sambandha*). An entity is considered to belong to someone only as long as one can assert possession over it according to prevailing norms. When wealth is lost or transferred, what is truly lost or transferred is one’s association or *sambandha* with the *svāmibhāva* or ownership. Hence, in the transfer of ownership, when one party gains possession, the other has to necessarily lose. This idea of reciprocal nature of ownership of wealth/ properties and its connection with declaring them as positive (*dhana*) and negative (*ṛṇa*) is beautifully brought out in the following passage:
एवं यस्मिन् वस्तुनि यस्य स्वस्वामिभावः सम्बन्धः तस्य तद्धनमिति व्यवह्रियते । तस्मिन् वैपरीत्यं तु परस्य स्वस्वामिभावः सम्बन्धः । अतो देवदत्तस्वामिके धने यावति यज्ञदत्तस्वामिकत्वं तावत् देवदत्तस्य ऋणमिति व्यवह्रियते ।
*evaṃ yasmin vastuni yasya svasvāmibhāvaḥ sambandhaḥ tasya taddhanamiti vyavahriyate | tasmin vaiparītyaṃ tu parasya svasvāmibhāvaḥ sambandhaḥ | ato devadattasvāmike dhane yāvati yajñadattasvāmikatvaṃ tāvat devadattasya ṛṇamiti vyavahriyate |*
Similarly, when an entity is considered as belonging to a person, it is deemed as his wealth; [and, therefore, positive]. The converse [i.e., *ṛṇa*] is the case when the ownership of that entity shifts to another person. Therefore, that portion of Devadatta’s wealth over which Yajñadatta acquires ownership [by property transfer] is to be declared as Devadatta’s debt [and, therefore, negative].
Similarly, an entity is described to be the wealth (*dhana*) of a person as long as he possesses ownership over it.

### 7.7 Upapatti using “*deśa*”—a geographic example to elucidate the concept of the negative

Kṛṣṇa Daivajña’s discussion on negative numbers using geographical locations to elucidate the concept is indeed captivating. The discussion explores the geographical relationship between three cities, all considered holy and pilgrimage spots. Of the three cities, two of them, Vārāṇasī and Prayāga are mentioned by names. The third one is simply described by the term ‘*pattana*’ and is stated to be on the banks of the Gaṅgā.
Furthermore in this discussion, Vārāṇasī is thought of as the centre or origin. The ‘*pattana*’ is 15 *yojanas* towards the east from Vārāṇasī and to the west of Vārāṇasī, in eight *yojanas*, is Prayāga. So, the distance of separation (*antara*) between the *pattana* and Prayāga is 23 *yojanas*. As the description presented by Kṛṣṇa Daivajña is quite elaborate, discursive and self-explanatory, with this brief introduction, we straight away present the original passage along with the translation without making any further remarks or attempts to paraphrase it at this stage:
श्रीविश्वेशितुः शम्भोः आनन्दकाननात् पुरन्दरदिशि पञ्चदशसु योजनेषु स्वर्गतरङ्गिणीतीरविलासि वरीवर्ति किलैकं पत्तनम् । वरुणदिशि वा अष्टसु योजनेषु इन्दीवरदलश्यामलापतङ्गतनयातङ्गचुम्बिभिः शरच्चन्द्रिकाधवलैः सुरनदीकल्लोलैः स्मृतहरिहरमूर्तिः आनन्दलहरीरनुभवन् जागर्ति तीर्थराजः प्रयागः ॥
*śrīviśveśituḥ śambhoḥ ānandakānanāt purandaradiśi pañcadaśasu yojaneṣu svargataraṅgiṇītīravilāsi varīvarti kilaikaṃ pattanam | varuṇadiśi vā aṣṭasu yojaneṣu indīvaradalaśyāmalapataṅgatanayātaraṅgacumbibhiḥ śaraccandrikādhavalaiḥ suranadīkallolaiḥ smṛtahariharamūrtiḥ ānandalaharīranubhavan jāgarti tīrtharājaḥ prayāgaḥ |*
तयोस्तु उच्चावचसकलजनव्यवहारसिद्धमस्ति त्रयो-विंशति-योजनात्मकम् अन्तरम् । तच्च योगं विना नोपपद्यते । अतो विजातीययोरन्तरे साध्ये योगः कर्तव्यः । परं स योगः पश्चिमः पूर्वो वा ? तत्र पत्तनात् प्रयागः कस्यां दिशीति विचारे तावत् आनन्दकाननात् प्रयागपर्यन्तम् अष्टयोजनात्मको देशः यथा पश्चिमः, तथा पत्तनादपि पश्चिम एव । किन्तु आनन्दकाननात् पत्तनपर्यन्तं पञ्चदशयोजनात्मको यः पूर्वदेशः स भवति पत्तनात् पश्चिम एव ।
एवं पत्तनात् प्रयागपर्यन्तं देशविचारे आनन्दवनपर्यन्तं पञ्चदशयोजनात्मकमेकं शकलं, ततः प्रयागावाधि द्वितीयम् अष्टयोजनात्मकं शकलद्वयस्य पश्चिमतवाज्जातः त्रयोविंशतियोजनत्मकः पश्चिमो देशः । एवं प्रयागात् पत्तनं कस्यां दिशीति विचारे प्रयागादानन्दवनपर्यन्तं देशशकलं विपरीतदिक्कं भवति ।

[^15]: In fact, all the clocks (digital or otherwise) have been designed to work in accordance with this apparent westward motion of the celestial sphere along with the celestial objects.

तयोस्तु उच्चावचसकलजनव्यवहारसिद्धमस्ति त्रयोविंशतियोजनात्मकम् अन्तरम् । तच्च योगं विना नोपपद्यते । अतो विजातीययोरन्तरे साध्ये योगः कर्तव्यः । परं स योगः पश्चिमः पूर्वो वा ? तत्र पत्तनात् प्रयागः कस्यां दिशीति विचारे तावत् आनन्दकाननात् प्रयागपर्यन्तम् अष्टयोजनात्मको देशः यथा पश्चिमः, तथा पत्तनादपि पश्चिमो भवति । किन्तु आनन्दकाननात् पत्तनपर्यन्तं पञ्चदशयोजनात्मको यः पूर्वदेशः स भवति पत्तनात् पश्चिम एव ॥
एवं पत्तनात् प्रयागपर्यन्तं देशविचारे आनन्दवनपर्यन्तं पञ्चदशयोजनात्मकमेकं शकलं, ततः प्रयागावधि द्वितीयम् अष्टयोजनात्मकम् । शकलद्वयस्य पश्चिमत्वाज्जातः त्रयोविंशतियोजनात्मकः पश्चिमो देशः । एवं प्रयागात् पत्तनं कस्यां दिशीति विचारे प्रयागादानन्दवनपर्यन्तं देशशकलं विपरीतदिक्कं भवति ॥
Fifteen *yojanas* east of (*purandaradiśi*) Vārāṇasī (*ānandakānanā*), wherein dwells Lord śiva (*śambhu*), the Controller of the Universe, indeed lies a city (*pattana*), nestled along the banks of the Gaṅgā (*svargataraṅgiṇī*). To the west (*varuṇadiśi*), in eight *yojanas*, at the confluence is the king of (i.e., foremost among) pilgrim sites, Prayāga, revelling in the reminiscences of Hari and Hara, evoked by the confluence of the waves of Yamunā (*pataṅgatanayā*: daughter of the Sun) with her deep complexion resembling the blue lotus (*indīvaradalaśyāmala*), [as she] mingles with the celestial Gaṅgā (*suranadī*), whose hues are as white as the autumnal moon (*śaraccandrikādhavalaiḥ*).
It is a commonplace knowledge established through the discourse of people of all classes (*uccāvacasakalajanavyavahārasiddham*) that the distance of separation (*antara*) between these cities (i.e., Prayāga and *pattana*) is $23\text{ yojanas}$. This would not be possible without the addition of [two] values. Thus to find the separation (*antara*) between [entities of] two genres (*vijātīyayoḥ*), addition must be performed. But is that addition eastward or westward? In discussing the direction of Prayāga from *pattana*, it is evident that as much as it is eight *yojanas* west from Vārāṇasī to Prayāga, so too, it is west with respect to *pattana*. However, [it is to be noted that] the distance of $15\text{ yojanas}$ eastwards from Vārāṇasī to *pattana* is to be considered westwards in relation to *pattana*.
Similarly, when discussing about the intervening space between *pattana* and Prayāga, there is a segment (*śakala*) of $15\text{ yojanas}$ that extends to Vārāṇasī, and another segment of eight *yojanas* that extends to Prayāga. Because both these segments are westwards, the entire stretch of $23\text{ yojana}$ is westwards. Similarly, when we discuss about which direction *pattana* is from Prayāga, the segment from Prayāga to Vārāṇasī is in the opposite direction.
The concepts described above can be better depicted as a figure to assist easy understanding. In Fig. 5, with respect to an observer $O$ at Vārāṇasī ($V$), there is a segment of $15\text{ yojanas}$ extending to *pattana* ($P_a$) in one direction and another segment of eight *yojanas* extending from Vārāṇasī to Prayāga ($P_r$) in the opposite direction. For the observer $O$, $OP_a$ is $+15$ and $OP_r$ is $-8$ (since it is in the opposite direction). The distance between $P_a$ and $P_r$ is obtained by finding the *antara* or difference between these two segments:

$$OP_a - (-OP_r) = P_a P_r.$$

That is,

$$15 - (-8) = 23.$$

To further elucidate Kṛṣṇa Daivajña’s exposition on negative numbers, it is essential to understand the dual meaning of the term ‘*antara*’ in Sanskrit. *Antara* signifies both the difference and the distance between two locations. This duality is fundamental in comprehending how Kṛṣṇa Daivajña conceptualises the subtraction of distances and the application of negative numbers. When he describes the distance between the *pattana* and Prayāga, he clarifies that the movement in opposite directions from a central point $O$ (Vārāṇasī) necessitates assigning one direction as positive and the opposite as negative. Thus, when determining the separation between these locations, the concept of subtracting a negative value naturally leads to a positive outcome, effectively illustrating the principle that subtracting a negative is equivalent to addition. This mathematical operation is not merely abstract but grounded in the physical reality of traversing distances in opposite directions. Therefore, when one moves $15\text{ yojanas}$ east from Vārāṇasī to the *pattana* and then $8\text{ yojanas}$ west to Prayāga, the total distance covered is $23\text{ yojanas}$, derived from the calculation $15 - (-8) = 23$. This explanation further underscores the practical application of negative numbers in ancient Indian mathematics, demonstrating how theoretical concepts are seamlessly integrated with tangible experiences.

## 7.8 Why subtracting a negative is equivalent to adding a positive?

Normally we learn in schools that two negative signs next to each other turn into a positive sign. This is usually learned as a mathematical rule without much of an explanation. The explanation that is at times offered is—think of removing a negative thought or reversing a loss that was incurred.

However, having illustrated how the direction of travel impacts the perception of distance (as positive and negative), the commentator here strives hard to drive in the point why $-(- \text{number} ) = + \text{number}$.
Proceeding with the finale of the discussion, he brilliantly elucidates why and how finding difference with a negative turns out to be a positive in the following para commencing with “*tathā ca*”, which means “therefore”. It is to be noted that this finale is purely based on the physical geographical description given above. In fact, it is to give this final punch such a detailed discussion was undertaken by Kṛṣṇa. Summarising the discussion he notes:
तथा च, यस्मादनन्तरं साध्यते तदवधिशकलं विपरीतदिक्कं भवतीत्यत उक्तम् -- “संशोध्यमानं स्वमृणत्वमेति स्वत्वं क्षयः” इति । एवं धनर्णयोरन्तरं प्रतिपादितम् । एवं धनयोरपि । तद्यथा -- एकः किल काशीतः पूर्वदिग्भागे दश योजनानि गतः, इतरोऽपि तस्मिन्नेव भागे सप्त योजनानि गतः । तयोश्चान्तरं योजनात्रयं सर्वजनसिद्धम् । तच्च दशयोजनगात् पश्चिमम् । सप्तयोजनगात् पूर्वम् ॥
*tathā ca yasmādanantaraṃ sādhyate tadavadhiśakalaṃ viparītadikkaṃ bhavatītyata uktam “saṃśodhyamānaṃ svamṛṇatvameti svatvaṃ kṣayaḥ” iti | evaṃ dhanarṇayorantaraṃ pratipāditam | evaṃ dhanayorapi | tadyathā - ekaḥ kila kāśītaḥ pūrvadigbhāge daśa yojanāni gataḥ, itaro’pi tasminneva bhāge sapta yojanāni gataḥ | tayoścāntaraṃ yojanatrayaṃ sarvajanasiddham | tacca daśayojanagāt paścimam | saptayojanagāt pūrvam |*
Therefore, the segment up to that reference point, beyond which the direction is determined from a different point to the first reference point, is in the opposite direction. Hence, it is said “*saṃśodhyamānaṃ svamṛṇatvameti svatvaṃ kṣayastadyutiruktavacca*.” Thus is the difference between positive and negative (*dhanarṇayorantaraṃ*) explained. This is the same for the positive as well. One travels $10\text{ yojanas}$ from Kāśī, and the other travels seven *yojanas*. It is known to all that their difference is three *yojanas*. And it is westward from the one who has travelled $10\text{ yojanas}$, and eastward from the one who has travelled seven *yojanas*.
This operation applies equally to positive directions. In Fig. 6 consider Vārāṇasī ($V$), also famously known as Kāśī, to be the initial point. If two travellers, $A$ and $B$, cover a distance of 10 and seven *yojanas* eastwards from Kāśī, then the difference in the magnitude of distance covered by them, $VA$ and $VB$ respectively, is clearly three *yojanas*. It is to be noted that these three *yojanas* is westward from the point of view of traveller $A$ who covered $10\text{ yojanas}$, and eastward from the point of view of traveller $B$ who covered seven *yojanas*.

## 7.9 Conjecture and evidence on identifying the ‘pattana’ of *Bījapallava* as modern-day Patna

Though not germane to the paper, it was a matter of curiosity for us to determine the identity of the city referred to as “*pattana*” in the *upapatti*.[^17] Based on preliminary analysis, we surmise that this city could possibly be Pāṭaliputra or modern-day Patna. The basis for this argument is as follows.
According to the text, the distance between Prayāgrāj and Vārāṇasī is stated as $8\text{ yojanas}$. Today we know that their distance is approximately $120\text{ km}$. This essentially means one *yojana* is estimated to be approximately $15\text{ km}$ according to Kṛṣṇa Daivajña. Additionally, the text mentions that the *pattana* is located $15\text{ yojanas}$ eastward of Vārāṇasī. The shortest distance (aerial) between Vārāṇasī and Pāṭaliputra is approximately $227\text{ km}$ and it aligns closely with the estimation of $15\text{ yojanas}$.
The text also describes that all three geographical locations are pilgrimage centres situated on the banks of the Gaṅgā. Fig. 7 depicts a route map of the river Gaṅgā, while Fig. 8 illustrates the positions of the three cities of Prayāgrāj, Vārāṇasī, and Patna on Google Maps.

## 8 Conclusion

Through these systematic, elaborate and coherent discussions, this excerpt from “*dhanarṇaṣaḍvidha*” in the *Bījapallava* attempts to present a comprehensive understanding of negative numbers and their multifaceted applications in both theoretical and practical domains. In particular, it is noteworthy that it provides a cue to the historical evolution of the notion of negative numbers within Indian mathematical thought. The discussion commences by introducing concepts rooted in accounting, such as debts and ownership, highlighting their relevance in understanding negative values. As the discussion progresses, the passage seamlessly transitions to arithmetic operations involving both positive and negative numbers, reminiscent of Brahmagupta’s seminal work. Furthermore, it delves into discussions on surds, akin to those found in Bhāskara’s *Bījagaṇita*, and explores geometric considerations, mirroring the mathematical inquiries of the *Līlāvatī*. Notably, the passage extends its examination to include applications of negative numbers within both terrestrial and celestial coordinate systems, aligning with the broader Indian astronomical tradition.
This comprehensive approach underscores the interconnectedness of mathematical concepts across various disciplines and highlights the enduring relevance of understanding negative numbers in diverse practical contexts. This study of a select excerpt from Kṛṣṇa Daivajña’s *Bījapallava* especially serves the following important purposes:
1. It highlights the invaluable role of commentaries in elucidating complex mathematical concepts.
2. It calls to attention the significance given to the concept of negative numbers in various branches of Indian knowledge systems.
3. It proposes that the “*pattana*” referred to in the excerpt is modern-day Patna.
Besides highlighting how negative arithmetic found applications in diverse fields, including accounting, astronomy and geography, the discussion also reflects an evolution of thought, indicating a progressive shift from practical, everyday, applications to theoretical abstraction. This evolution mirrors the broader development of mathematical thinking regarding negative numbers in Indian mathematical thought, showcasing how early inquiries rooted in practical scenarios laid the groundwork for more complex theoretical frameworks. Moreover, Kṛṣṇa Daivajña’s detailed discussion not only highlights his proficiency in simplifying abstract ideas but also his dedication to ensure that these ideas resonate with the readers.
