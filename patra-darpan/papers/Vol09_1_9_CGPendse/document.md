# COPERNICAN HELIOCENTRIC THEORY AND INDIAN ASTRONOMY

**C. G. PENDSE**
**Maharashtra Association for the Cultivation of Science**
**Poona 4**
Copernicus started the process of the renovation of Astronomy by creating a model for the solar system by adapting the heliocentric theory. The process was completed by Kepler; he utilized Tycho Brahe’s observational data. Kepler founded his model for the solar system containing his famous laws of planetary motion. The idea of the heliocentric theory had been mooted several centuries before Copernicus but had remained inoperative. The ancient Indian Astronomy contained the germ of the heliocentric theory in Āryabhaṭa’s treatise *Āryabhaṭīyam*, which had been translated into Latin in the thirteenth century A.D. and might therefore have been accessible to Copernicus. The seniority (or antiquity) and originality of ancient Indian astronomy are certain, as can be seen from the writings of Playfair and Craufurd. Āryabhaṭa’s treatise shows an awareness, amazing for his time, on his part of the notion of the relativity of motion and of rotation. Āryabhaṭa’s original idea was snuffed out by his successors, particularly Brahmagupta.
The five-hundredth anniversary of the birth of Nicolaus Copernicus (b. 1473 Feb. 19, d. 1543 May 24) is an occasion to sober us. The five centuries have witnessed a tremendous development of Science (including astronomy) and Technology; and the fields of mathematics, physics and astronomy were virtually initiated in a new mode of development by the introduction by Copernicus of the heliocentric model for the Solar System. The greatness of his work is certain and universally acknowledged. The object of this essay is to recapitulate briefly what service Copernicus rendered to astronomy by introducing the heliocentric theory and the sequel to it at the hands of Tycho Brahe (1546-1601) and Johann Kepler (1571-1630) and then to mention the work of Indian astronomers before Copernicus, especially as regards the heliocentric theory.

## (I)

### COPERNICUS, TYCHO BRAHE AND KEPLER

In his masterly and extensive article “Astronomy” in the *Encyclopaedia Britannica*, 9th edition, Vol. 2 (1875), pp. 744-823, Richard Anthony Proctor (1837-1888) gave in Part I (pp. 744-763) a succinct account of the history of astronomy.
Copernicus started the period of the overthrow the Ptolemaic system and the renovation of the science of astronomy. In the model for the solar system proposed by Copernicus, “the heaven composed of stars perfectly at rest, occupies the remotest bounds of space, then the orbit of Saturn, next Jupiter, Mars, the Earth (accompanied by its moon), Venus and Mercury, and, lastly, the Sun immovable at the centre”. The orbit of each planet was circular and the Sun was within each orbit but not in the centre of any of them, the Earth rotating

---
relative to the stellar frame and revolving in a circular orbit around the Sun. The merit of the Copernican model was its simplicity. The model which Copernicus had to adopt explained the data which then existed; the pattern of uniform motion in the circular orbit was ordained by tradition. The weakness of this model lies in the fact that the Sun is not in the centre of any orbit so that “he had no other office to perform than to distribute light and heat; being excluded from any influence on the system, he became as it were a stranger to all the motions”. All the same the Copernican model was a great step forward, despite its imperfections.
Copernicus was followed by Tycho Brahe. Tycho Brahe collected a vast amount of observational data, but favoured a ‘semi-geocentric’ model. In Tycho Brahe’s model the Earth rotates relative to the stellar frame but is otherwise at rest.
Tycho Brahe was followed by Kepler. Kepler used the observational data bequeathed to him by Tycho Brahe. Kepler, after a stupendous, prolonged and awe-inspiring labour and effort, framed his justly celebrated laws of planetary motion and placed the heliocentric system on a firm basis. The important break with tradition made by Kepler was that, in his model, the planetary orbits round the Sun are ellipses having a common focus occupied by the stationary Sun.
In sum, Copernicus had the intellectual courage to adopt the doctrine of a stationary Sun and a rotating and revolving Earth despite the pressure of traditional and ecclesiastical dogmas, Tycho Brahe had the intellectual courage to accept the doctrine of a rotating earth and Kepler had the two-fold intellectual courage to adopt the heliocentric idea plus not to shun elliptical orbits.
Two important points regarding Kepler’s success have been mentioned by Plummer: H. C. Plummer, “*Dynamical Astronomy*” (Cambridge, 1918, p. 2). First, Tycho Brahe’s observational data utilized by Kepler were of just the right order of accuracy: they enabled Kepler to judge the different hypotheses, but they were not “so refined as to involve the problem in a maze of unmanageable detail”. Secondly, Kepler’s knowledge of the Greek mathematicians, particularly of the works of Apollonius, stood him in good stead.
In terms of the doctrine of the relativity of motion, the import of the transition from the geocentric theory to the heliocentric theory for the solar system is that of change from one frame of reference to another frame of reference more suitable or efficient for coordination, analysis and synthesis in respect of the observational data involved. The mental difficulties for that transition involved the fact that there is no direct ocular demonstration of either the motion of the Earth relative to the Sun or the rotation of the Earth relative to the stellar firmament.
Proctor’s judicial assessment of the importance of the work of Copernicus may be noted with interest. The gist of that assessment is that—
---
(i) Copernicus deserves a high place among philosophers for reviving the already existing heliocentric theory in the face of the prejudices, hostile authority and ignorance of his times, but
(ii) his title to the great fame brought to him by his actual services to astronomy may admit of doubt.
The idea of the heliocentric theory had been mooted long before Copernicus. Pythagoras (born early in sixth century B.C. — died towards the beginning of the fifth century B.C.) is reputed to have preached the heliocentric idea to his chosen pupils, though he publicly taught the geocentric theory; but he left no records on the subject. Aristarchus (who lived in the third century B.C.), in his treatise *On the Magnitudes and Distances of the Sun and Moon*, whose Latin translation was published, had accepted the doctrine regarding the earth’s motion. Hence, it seems certain that Copernicus had learnt about the heliocentric doctrine from his forerunners. B. V. Subbarayappa in his essay “India’s Contribution to the History of Science” on pp. 47-65 in the collective work *India’s Contribution to World Thought and Culture* (Vivekananda Rock Memorial Committee, Madras, Oct. 1970) has written (p. 47): “*Āryabhaṭa’s Āryabhaṭīyam* was translated into Latin in Italy in the thirteenth century A.D.”. Copernicus might have been influenced by this Latin translation of *Āryabhaṭīyam*, which has a germ of the heliocentric theory, as is submitted below.

## (II)

### INDIAN ASTRONOMY AND ĀRYABHAṬA

It is now necessary to notice the contribution to Astronomy from India, particularly in this context.
The eminent mathematician and natural philosopher John Playfair (1748-1819), known for Playfair’s axiom in Elementary Geometry, published a memoir “On the Astronomy of the Brahmins” (1790) in the *Transactions of the Royal Society of Edinburgh*; in the words of Lord Jeffrey, who wrote a biographical memoir of Playfair, this paper “attracted very general attention both in Europe and in Asia and gave rise to much discussion and research”. Playfair wrote a sequel in the form of the paper “Observations on the Trigonometrical Tables of the Brahmins” (1797).
Playfair’s 1790 paper has been extensively quoted by Quentin Craufurd (1743-1819) in his “Researches Concerning the Laws, Theology, Learning, and Commerce of Ancient and Modern India” (London, 1817), in Chapter VIII (“On the Astronomy and other Sciences of the Hindus”). Craufurd has given a remarkable account of the astronomical and mathematical work that had been done in India.
---
These very important publications of Playfair and Craufurd appear to be completely unknown to modern Indian and Indological Scholars. Shankar Balkrishna Dixit (1853-1898) makes a reference to a paper by Playfair in “Asiatic Researches” but not to either Craufurd’s book or Playfair’s papers mentioned above, in his justly famous Marathi book (1896, reissued 1931) on the history of Indian astronomy. (An English translation of Dixit’s book is being published in portions, the first portion appearing in 1968). It is fortunate that Playfair’s 1790 paper has been reprinted in the book *Indian Science and Technology in the Eighteenth Century* by Dharampal (New Delhi: Impex India, 1971).
Craufurd’s account shows that the astronomical and mathematical work done in India was remarkable and that the astronomical tables and rules were strikingly accurate for those times. The value $\frac{3927}{1250} (= 3.1416)$ for the ratio of the circumference to the diameter of a circle was known to those people. The origin of Indian astronomy has been traced to 4300 years B.C.
The work of the ancient Indian astronomers has been held to have been entirely original. How and when the intellectual originality and virility of those forefathers gave place to intellectual passivity or supineness and conservatism noticed by others in their successors or descendents a few centuries ago could not be ascertained.
Craufurd and Playfair have not been mentioned by Lokamanya Bal Gangadhar Tilak (1856-1920) in his important treatise *The Orion or Researches into the Antiquity of the Vedas* (1893). One cannot help feeling that Tilak’s thinking would have been substantially influenced and his thesis would have been further strengthened, if he had known about and been able to see Craufurd’s book or Playfair’s 1790 paper.
Proctor, in the paragraph “Indians” in the history of astronomy in his article mentioned above, has spoken of “the people who now blindly follow its precepts without understanding its principles” and, without committing himself to any opinion, has mentioned one opinion (basides two other opinions) that astronomy was entirely given to those Indians by persons from other countries. Proctor has not mentioned the writings of either Playfair or Craufurd.
Hence, the assessment given by Craufurd appears to be accurate.
Before turning to the extant ancient Indian astronomical writings of identified authors beginning with Āryabhaṭa I, it is necessary to mention the work of their forerunners. This work was handed down in the form of five *siddhāntas*—*Sūryasiddhānta*, *Somasiddhānta*, *Vasiṣṭhasiddhānta*, *Romakasiddhānta* and (*Śākalyokta*) *Brāhmasiddhānta*—which incorporate astronomical lore. It is difficult to say anything definite about their epochs or their actual authors. In
---
the case of *Vasiṣṭhasiddhānta*, its epoch has been calculated by Pandit Bhagwan Das Pathak in his not widely known book “Hindu-Aryan Astronomy and Antiquity of Aryan Race” (printed at the Garhwali Press, Dehra Dun; published by the author; 1920); he places (p. 55) the beginning of Vasiṣṭha’s epoch at 1905 B.C. (which is over a thousand years before Pythagoras).
Now comes the series of eminent ancient Indian astronomers Āryabhaṭa I (b. A.D. 476-d. ?), Brahmagupta (b. 498—d. 665), Āryabhaṭa II (b. ?-d ?, flourished around A.D. 960), Bhāskarācārya (b. 1114—d. ?) and several others.
Āryabhaṭa I was held in great respect throughout the centuries. Prabodhchandra Sengupta, in his essay “Aryabhata: The Father of Indian Epicyclic Astronomy” (*Journal of the Department of Letters*, Vol. 18, Calcutta University Press, 1928), has given a critical account and appraisal of the work of Āryabhaṭa I. Prabodhchandra Sengupta has also published an English translation “The *Āryabhaṭīyam* (Translation)” (*Journal of the Department of Letters*, Vol. 16, Calcutta University Press, 1927) of Āryabhaṭa’s work. (*Āryabhaṭīyam* was called *Āryasiddhānta* by some other astronomers).
Ancient Indian Astronomy has been based on the geocentric theory. But it is important to notice that the heliocentric theory did *not* escape the attention of Āryabhaṭa I and this aspect is now considered. His work is in metrical form and concise and does not give explanatory amplifications, which a prose composition might probably have contained. It is necessary to get an idea of what his statement meant to his contemporaries and successors from their commentaries or strictures on his work, particularly the remarks made by Brahmagupta.
The translations, given by Sengupta, of stanzas 9 and 10 in Section IV (*Gola* or Spheres) of *Āryabhaṭīyam* are:—
“9. Just as a man going east by a boat sees a stationary thing as if it is going west, so do the fixed stars appear to be going straight westward at *Laṅkā* (the equator).”
“10. The frame of the constellations with all the planets daily moves straight westward at *Laṅkā* (the equator) being thrown that way by the wind called *Pravāha* for producing their daily rising and settings”.
*Laṅkā* means here the equator.
Stanza 10 is incompatible with Stanza 9. Says Sengupta regarding stanza 10: “Āryabhaṭa is apparently contradicting his statement in the previous stanza; he seems here unable to construct a new idea of motion in place of the old equable motion of heavenly bodies which he states in Sec. I, 6. It is further not impossible that this stanza has undergone drastic emendations from his commentators.” It seems that stanza 10 was either due to a downright interpolation or an utter

mangling of what Āryabhaṭa actually wrote, at the hands of a hostile commentator or copyist.
Āryabhaṭa’s stanza 9 was fated to be written off by his successors. Brahmagupta bitterly criticised Āryabhaṭa in many respects. A detailed account of the work of Brahmagupta and his criticisms of Āryabhaṭa is given in the monograph “Introduction: A Critical Study of Brahmagupta and his Works” by Prof. Satya Prakash in Vol. I of the edition in four volumes of Brahmagupta’s *Brāhmasphuṭa siddhānta* published by the Indian Institute of Astronomical and Sanskrit Research (New Delhi, 1966). On p. 9 of the monograph Satya Prakash gives quotations showing how Brahmagupta rejected the idea of the rotation of the earth put forward by Āryabhaṭa.
Āryabhaṭa’s stanza 9 shows a breadth of outlook unusual or even unique for his times and for centuries which followed him. The stanza contains the germ of the notion of the relativity of motion and also of rotation; and this notion suggests the heliocentric system with the earth rotating in the stellar frame. Fully to appreciate the intellectual feat of Āryabhaṭa involved in this stanza, it is necessary and worth while to recall the debate in dynamics regarding the relativity of motion and particularly rotational motion. Newton, in his *Principia* (1687), admitted the relativity of position but postulated the absoluteness of direction; and this scheme of things was reiterated in the nineteenth century by Thomson and Tait in their famous *Treatise on Natural Philosophy* and by Maxwell in his famous little book *Matter and Motion*. Mach, however, in the last century, put forward the idea of the relativity of rotation; and when the English translation *The Science of Mechanics* (1893) of his book, containing (inter alia) his idea of the relativity of rotation, appeared, it was reviewed in *Nature*; and the reviewer’s remarks regarding rotation led to a debate in the columns of that journal: *Nature*, Vol. 51 (Nov. 1894-Apr. 1895). That debate boiled down to the question of how to decide between absoluteness and relativity, and it also showed how differences of temperamental or intellectual moulds affect the decision. In that debate Love gave out the pithy aphorism “It is not legitimate to suppose that two relatives make one absolute”.
The treatment accorded to Āryabhaṭa’s kinematical idea in his stanza 9 by his successors shows how an important idea can remain suppressed for a long time. This is a sobering thought.

# THE PAÑCASIDDHĀNTIKĀ OF VARĀHAMIHIRA (1)

**KRIPA SHANKAR SHUKLA**
Department of Mathematics, University of Lucknow, Lucknow
The *Pañcasiddhāntikā* of Varāhamihira is one of the most important sources for the history of Hindu astronomy before the time of Āryabhaṭa I (b. 476 A.D.). Two editions of this work (both furnished with English translation and commentary) have appeared, one in 1889 under the editorship of G. Thibaut and S. Dvivedi, and the other in two parts in 1970 and 1971 under the editorship of O. Neugebauer and D. Pingree. But even now the contents of the work are at places not correctly understood. The object of the proposed series of papers is to deal with certain passages of the work which have not been properly understood so far. In the present paper which is the first of the series I propose to deal with four topics, viz. (i) criticism of Viṣṇucandra and *Romaka* by Pauliśa, (ii) the declination table of Varāhamihira, (iii) the fifth correction for Mercury and Venus in the Old *Sūryasiddhānta*, and (iv) a traditional correction of the *Pauliśa* school for the longitude of the Moon’s ascending node.

D. Pingree, whose edition of the *Pañcasiddhāntikā* appeared in 1970, has translated the above verses as follows:
“32. If the beginning (*pratipatti*) occurs when there is separation of *tithi* and *nakṣatra*, then it is good. But it is not so in a *bhadrā tithi* and Viṣṇu’s *nakṣatra* (*Śravaṇa*): for thus does the world disappear.
33. There is not simultaneously everywhere a rising of the Sun or its setting. In what place is its setting? From that basis they know what has passed of the day.
34. This is arrived at from a method; there is no quickness in so very long a time. Look at its (the world’s) destruction in $68550$ years.
35. Taking the *Romaka ahargaṇa* as the basis, let one calculate (the longitudes of) the Sun and the Moon on the full moon (*tithi*) of *Caitra*; on the ninth (*tithi*) the *nakṣatra* is *Āditya* (*Punarvasu*).
36. The *śrauta* and *smārta* regulations depend on time; because a twice-born through offending them is a *prāyaścittī* (i.e., he has to perform propitiatory rites), therefore he studies this (i.e., time).
37. Whatever twice-born men, knowing a bad *karaṇa*, say that (astronomical) calculations are inaccurate and false, they, together with the makers of bad *karaṇas*, instantly make their homes in hell.
38. (But) one who knows accurate calculations of the Sun, and so on, obtains *dharma*, wealth, and praise in this world.”
O. Neugebauer and D. Pingree have supplemented the above translation by the following commentary:
“These verses are evidently based on some obscure speculation in *Romakasiddhānta* about the duration of creation.
The separation of *tithi* and *nakṣatra* presumably means that at the first *tithi* of the month the Moon is not in the first *nakṣatra*, *Aśvinī*; this separation is supposed to be an auspicious *muhūrta* for the *pratipatti*, i.e. the beginning of any action (or the beginning of creation?). However, if on a *bhadrā tithi* (the 2nd, 7th, or 12th in any *pakṣa*) the Moon is in *Śravaṇa* (Sagittarius $10^\circ$ to $23^\circ 20'$), the *muhūrta* is inauspicious. The inauspiciousness arises from the fact that the creation ceases at such a *yuga*, i.e. when the conjunction of the Sun and Moon (the first *tithi*) occurs in *Uttarāṣāḍha*, i.e. at the winter solstice. This is reminiscent of Hellenistic speculations regarding a “world-year”.
The $68550$ years in verse 34 is derived from the *Romakasiddhānta*; it is equal to $24 \times 19 \times 150 + 150$, where $19 \times 150 = 2850$ years is the Romaka’s *yuga* (cf. ch. 1, vs. 15). The significance of this computation is obscure.
The meaning of verse 35 also defies comprehension. Dikshit has indeed demonstrated that, by the elements of Varāhamihira’s *Sūryasiddhānta*, the *Caitra* whose *pratipad* is used as epoch in this *karaṇa* is *pūrṇimānta*; but there is no reason to compute the longitudes of the Sun and Moon for the *pūrṇimā* of that month. Moreover, at *Caitrapūrṇimā* the Moon must be close to Libra $0^\circ$ so that the Moon on the ninth *tithi* is far from *Punarvasu* (Gemini $20^\circ$ to Cancer $3^\circ 20'$). The reference to *Punarvasu* rather suggests an ecpyrosis at the summer solstice as we had a cataclysm at the winter solstice (vs. 32), but the text as it stands does not allow us to arrive at this interpretation.”
The above translation and commentary clearly shows that Neugebauer and Pingree have not understood the real import of the text and are guided by conjectures only. They are indeed off the track. The verses in question, in fact, constitute a criticism of Viṣṇucandra and Romaka whose *tithis* and *nakṣatras* were showing a wide divergence from the actual ones. The following modified translation would make the contents quite clear:
32. If the end (*cheda*) or commencement (*pratipatti*) of *tithi* and *nakṣatra* is as it should be, then it is good. But that of Shri Viṣṇu(candra) [^1] is not so; even then people (instead of discarding him) revert to him.
33. There is not simultaneously everywhere (on the same meridian) a rising of the Sun or its setting. In what meridian (lit. place) is its setting? From that basis they say what has passed of the day. [^2]
34. From the tradition (of the *śāstras*) it is learnt that there is no decrease in time even after a lapse of enormous time. (But) look at its (the world’s) destruction in $68550$ years (advocated by **Romaka**).
35. For those who calculate (the longitudes of) the Sun and Moon on the full-moon day of *Caitra*, taking the Romaka *ahargaṇa* as the basis, it is the ninth (*tithi*), and the *Punarvasu nakṣatra* (and not the full-moon *tithi* and the *Citrā nakṣatra* as it should be).
36. The *śrauta* and *smārta* regulations depend on time; because a twice-born through offending them is a *prāyaścittī* (i.e. he has to perform propitiatory rites), therefore he studies this (time-ascertaining science of astronomy).
37. Those twice-born who, having studied bad *karaṇas*, declare inaccurate and false calculations, they, together with the authors of bad *karaṇas*, instantly make their homes in hell.
38. (But) one who knows accurate calculations of the Sun, etc., obtains *dharma*, wealth, and praise in the world.
This translation is self-explanatory and on the basis of it one can easily draw the following conclusions:
(1) In the time of Pauliśa Viṣṇucandra’s edition of the *Vasiṣṭhasiddhānta* was not yielding correct *tithis* and *nakṣatras*. But Viṣṇucandra was a popular astronomer and had a great following.
(2) Calculations based on the *Romakasiddhānta* were showing an error of six *tithis* and seven *nakṣatras*.
(3) Pauliśa, like Āryabhaṭa I, believed that time had no beginning or end, but Romaka held the contrary view.
Criticism of Viṣṇucandra and Romaka in the *Pauliśasiddhānta* further shows that *Pauliśasiddhānta* was written subsequent to the *Siddhāntas* of Viṣṇucandra and Romaka. The statement of Varāhamihira, viz.

in ch. 1, vs. 10 is thus significant and should be understood to mean:
“This is according to the *Romakasiddhānta*; so it is also according to the *Pauliśasiddhānta* which is not much old.”
This is the natural and straightforward meaning of the above hemistich.
Occurrence of criticism of Viṣṇucandra, Romaka, Vijayanandī and Pradyumna in the writing of a person like Varāhamihira shows that Brahmagupta’s critical remarks against them are not totally baseless and unjustified. Sarcastic remarks against the Romakas are also found in the writings of Bhāskara I who was a contemporary of Brahmagupta. It is significant that Pauliśa has not been criticized by Brahmagupta or others.

## 3. THE FIFTH CORRECTION FOR MERCURY AND VENUS IN THE OLD SŪRYASIDDHĀNTA.

In the old *Sūryasiddhānta* school, the true longitude of the superior planets (Mars, Jupiter and Saturn) were obtained by applying the following four corrections:
For obtaining the true longitude of the planet's apogee:
(1) Half *śīghraphala* to the longitude of the planet's apogee (reversely).
(2) Half *mandaphala* to the corrected longitude of the planet's apogee (reversely).
For obtaining the true longitude of the planet:
(3) Entire *mandaphala* (calculated with the help of the true longitude of the planet's apogee) to the mean longitude of the planet.

(4) Entire *śīghraphala* to the corrected mean longitude (called **true-mean longitude**) of the planet.
In the case of the inferior planets (Mercury and Venus) a fifth correction (called *pañcama saṃskāra*) was applied in addition to the above-mentioned four corrections. In the case of Mercury this correction was calculated and applied in accordance with the following rule:
Subtract the longitude of the Sun's apogee from the longitude of Mercury's *śīghrocca*; multiply the $R$ sine of the resulting difference by the Sun's epicycle and divide by 360; the quotient gives the fifth correction for Mercury. 5. Put down the degrees of the ascending node increased by 36 (or 26?) minutes. (Operate) with the degrees of the difference between this and (the longitude of) the Moon; if they are within $13^\circ$, there is an eclipse of the Moon, and if within $8^\circ$, an eclipse of the Sun.”
A close scrutiny reveals that the translation of the first line of each of the above two verses as given by both Thibaut and Pingree is not correct, because
राहो: सषट्कतिलकं अंशं हित्वा
actually means “having subtracted one degree together with thirty-six minutes.” The above two verses should therefore be translated as follows:
2. One degree and thirty-six minutes having been subtracted from (the longitude of) the Moon’s ascending node, if the degrees arising from the difference of that (corrected longitude of Moon’s ascending node) and (the longitude of) the Moon are within thirteen, there is an eclipse (of the Moon), and if within fifteen, there is a darkening of that (Moon).

5. One degree and thirty-six minutes having been subtracted from (the longitude of) the Moon’s ascending node, if the degrees arising from the difference of that (corrected longitude of the Moon’s ascending node) and (the longitude of) the Moon are within thirteen, there is an eclipse of the Moon, and if within eight, an eclipse of the Sun.
The correctness of this translation is confirmed by the fact that the correction of $1^\circ 36'$ to the longitude of the Moon’s ascending node was in regular use amongst the followers of the *Khaṇḍakhādyaka* of Brahmagupta (b. 598). Although this correction was not mentioned in the *Khaṇḍakhādyaka*, the followers of the *Khaṇḍakhādyaka* made use of it as a traditional correction. The following verse occurring in a manuscript[^5] of the *Khaṇḍakhādyaka* in the collection of the Akhila Bharatiya Sanskrit Parishad, Lucknow, throws light on this tradition:
पातस्य सम्प्रदायाद्विशोध्येकमंशकं लिप्ताः ।
षट्त्रिंशत्स्फुटपातस्स भवति सर्वत्र साधने योग्यः ॥[^6]
i.e., “From (the longitude of) the Moon’s ascending node one should, following the tradition, subtract one degree and thirty-six minutes. Then is obtained the true (longitude of the) Moon’s ascending node, which is fit for use in all calculations.”
This verse is also mentioned in Bina Chatterjee’s edition of the *Khaṇḍakhādyaka* (Vol. II, p. 8, footnote, lines 10-11), where it runs as follows:
पातस्य सम्प्रदायाद्विशोध्येकमंशकं लिप्ताः ।
षड्विंशतिः स्फुटपातः स भवति सर्वत्र साधने योग्यः ॥
The reading **षड्विंशतिः** given here is undoubtedly wrong, firstly because in the same edition elsewhere[^7] the correction in question has been expressly stated as “ninety-six minutes” (**षण्णवतिः कलाः**) and secondly because the reading **षड्विंशतिः** does not fit in the metre of the verse. With this reading the third quarter of the verse contains 13 syllabic instants (*mātrās*), whereas in fact there should be 12 syllabic instants only.
It is noteworthy that the commentators of the *Khaṇḍakhādyaka* have prescribed the use of the above correction if the longitude of the Moon’s ascending node was calculated according to the rule given in the *Pūrva-Khaṇḍakhādyaka* and have forbidden its use if the longitude of the Moon’s ascending node was calculated according to the rule given in the *Uttara-Khaṇḍakhādyaka*. Thus writes the commentator Pṛthūdaka (864):
“तस्मात् षण्णवति: कला: संशोध्या: सम्प्रदायावच्छेदा: । पारम्पर्येणैव कृते कर्मणोयश्चन्द्रपातो भवति ।”[^8]
“उत्तरकृताच्चन्द्रपातात् षण्णवति: कला न शोध्या इति ।”[^9]
THE PAÑCASIDDHĀNTIKĀ OF VARĀHAMIHIRA 75
i.e. “From that (i.e. the longitude of the Moon’s ascending node calculated according to *Pūrva-Khaṇḍakhādyaka*) one should subtract the traditional correction of 96 minutes. This correction having been applied in accordance with the tradition, the longitude of the Moon’s ascending node becomes fit for use in calculations.”
“From the longitude of the Moon’s ascending node calculated from (the rule given in) the *Uttara Khaṇḍakhādyaka*, 96 minutes should not be subtracted.”
So also writes the commentator Bhaṭṭotpala (968):
“अंश: सषट्कतिलक: शोध्य: पातस्य पूर्वस्य ।”[^10]
“अनेन प्रकारेण कृतस्य चन्द्रपातस्य षण्णवति: कला न शोध्या: ।”[^11]
i.e., “One degree together with thirty-six minutes should be subtracted from (the longitude of) the Moon’s ascending node calculated according to *Pūrva-Khaṇḍakhādyaka*.”
“Ninety-six minutes should not be subtracted from the longitude of the Moon’s ascending node if it is calculated by this method (of the *Uttara Khaṇḍakhādyaka*).”
Note that the language used by Bhaṭṭotpala in his first statement is exactly similar to that used by Varāhamihira.
One may ask the question: How is it that the correction prescribed for application to the longitude of the Moon’s ascending node by the *Pauliśasiddhānta* of Varāhamihira was regarded as traditional by the followers of the *Pūrva-Khaṇḍakhādyaka*? The reason seems to be that at a certain stage the followers of the *Pauliśasiddhānta* fell in line with the followers of the *Āryabhaṭasiddhānta*. They revised the old *Pauliśasiddhānta* in the light of the teachings of the *Āryabhaṭasiddhānta* and adopted the *Pūrva Khaṇḍakhādyaka* (which was based on the *Āryabhaṭasiddhānta*) as a work of their own school. Quotations from the *Pauliśasiddhānta* that are found to occur in the writings of Pṛthūdaka (864), Bhaṭṭotpala (968), Āmarāja (c. 1200), and the Persian scholar Al-Bīrūnī (b. 973) leave no room to doubt that the revised *Pauliśasiddhānta* was in conformity with the teachings of Āryabhaṭa I under the midnight day-reckoning. It is noteworthy that the commentators of the *Khaṇḍakhādyaka* have shown special preference to *Pauliśasiddhānta* in their citations from the ancient *Siddhāntas*.
The followers of the *Uttara Khaṇḍakhādyaka* did not apply the above correction because the *Uttara Khaṇḍakhādyaka* conformed to the teachings of the *Brāhmasphuṭasiddhānta* of Brahmagupta and such a correction was not prescribed there.
**Note.** The correction of $-96'$ for the Moon’s ascending node shows its appearance in the school of Āryabhaṭa I under the sunrise day-reckoning also.
