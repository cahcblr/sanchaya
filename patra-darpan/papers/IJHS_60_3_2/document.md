### Abstract

This study explores the astronomical significance of 13–15 April, celebrated as the new year in parts of South Asia and Southeast Asia. An analemma, representing the Sun’s position in the sky at the same time each day over a year, reveals a figure-eight curve with intersections occurring around 13–15 April. By analysing the analemma curve and utilizing online tools to calculate the Sun path, this research investigates the alignment between the 13–15 April new year and the celestial phenomenon. The study examines the correlation between the Sun’s position directly overhead at Rameswaram, a strategic location, and the selection of 13–15 April as the New Year date. This study provides valuable insights into the ancient astronomical considerations that influenced cultural traditions and calendar systems.
**Keywords** Analemma · Sun path · Astronomy · New year · Cultural tradition

## 1 Introduction

The movement of the Sun in the sky is a fascinating phenomenon that has captivated humans throughout history. The Sun’s path in the sky, known as the Sun’s apparent diurnal motion, varies depending on the observer’s location on the planet. Throughout the year, the Sun’s position in the sky also shifts gradually due to the tilt of the Earth’s axis and its elliptical orbit around the Sun. Before the advent of satellite navigation, sunrise time and noon time were used to determine the latitude and longitude for navigation.
The period from 13 to 15 April is celebrated as a new year in large parts of South and Southeast Asia. In India, it is known as *Puthandu* in Tamil Nadu, *Vishu* in Kerala, *Bishuba Sankranti* in Odisha, *Pohela Boishakh* in Bengal, *Jur Sital* in Mithila, *Baisakhi* in Punjab, Haryana, and Jammu, *Basoa* in Himachal, *Bikhoti* in Uttarakhand, *Rongali Bihu* in Assam, *Sangken* in Arunachal, *Buisu Tring* in Tripura, and *Aluth Awurudda* and *Puthandu* in Sri Lanka. In Southeast Asia, it is known as *Songkran* in Thailand and Laos, *Sangkranta* in Cambodia, and *Thingyan* in Myanmar (Chatterjee, 1998; Kumar, 2023).[^1]
The new year dates in India are published in the traditional Hindu calendar known as *Paňcāṅga* or *Paňcāṅgam* (Sewell, 1995; Fuller, 1980; Abhyankar, 1993). The traditional astronomical explanation for the current 13–15 April date is that the Sun enters the constellation Aries (English) or *Meśa* (Sanskrit). Around two thousand years ago, the New Year date was set to coincide with the vernal equinox. Due to a gradual wobble in Earth’s rotational axis—similar to the motion of a spinning top—the position of the equinoxes slowly shifts westward along the ecliptic at a rate of approximately 1° every 72 years. This phenomenon, known as the precession of the equinoxes, completes a full cycle roughly every 26,000 years. While ancient Indian astronomers may not have understood the mechanics of axial precession, they observed long-term patterns by comparing celestial alignments with seasonal events over generations. Temples and observatories were often precisely aligned with the sunrise or sunset on solstices and equinoxes. Over time, the gradual misalignment of these structures with the originally observed star positions revealed the slow celestial drift—a phenomenon we now recognize as precession.
The Indian *nirayaṇa* calendar system is a sidereal (star-based) method of astronomical calculation. Unlike the tropical (*sayana*) system used in Western astronomy, which is based on the moving equinoxes, the *nirayaṇa* system uses a fixed background of stars and constellations as its reference. The term *nirayaṇa* means "without *ayaṇa*"—that is, without accounting for the precession (shift) of the equinoxes. In this system, the twelve zodiac signs begin from a fixed point, usually 0° Aries (*Meśa*), which is defined relative to prominent stars such as Spica (*Citrā*) or Zeta Piscium (*Revatī*). Spica, also known as $\alpha$-Virginis, is in the Virgo constellation.
The most widely adopted correction in sidereal astrology is the Lahiri *ayanāṃśa*, which places 0° Aries exactly 180° opposite Spica, effectively marking the start of the sidereal zodiac around 285 CE. Spica has held significance across several ancient cultures—Babylonian, Greek, Arabic, and Indian—owing to its proximity to the ecliptic, making it a useful marker for observing the precession of the equinoxes. In the *nirayaṇa* system, the Sun’s entry into each zodiac sign marks the beginning of a solar month, and the Sun’s entry into Aries (*Meśa*) is considered the start of the sidereal solar year.[^2]
The start of the Hindu new year count was 3104 BCE after a special planetary alignment, according to Abhyankar (1993). According to tradition, it was believed this was the start of the *Kaliyuga* and the death of Lord *Kṛṣṇa*. The Tamil literature of *Purananuru*, written between the first and third centuries CE, mentions the movement of the star constellations in March before the new year (Hart, 1999).
The Gregorian calendar, now the most widely used system globally, was introduced by Pope Gregory XIII in 1582 to address errors in the earlier Julian calendar, which had been in effect since 45 BCE. BCE stands for Before Common Era, previously referred to as Before Christ, indicating the years before the traditional birth year of Jesus Christ. The Julian calendar slightly overestimated the solar year’s length by approximately 11 min, causing a gradual shift in the timing of the equinoxes and key dates such as Easter. To correct this drift, Pope Gregory implemented a reform that involved skipping 10 days—so October 4, 1582, was immediately followed by October 15, 1582—and modifying the leap year rule. Under the new rule, a year would be a leap year if divisible by 4, but century years would only be leap years if also divisible by 400. This adjustment greatly improved the calendar’s accuracy, keeping it more precisely in sync with the Earth’s revolution around the Sun (Gordon and Moyer, 1982). The Persian New Year, known as *Nowruz* based on Solar Hijri calendar, is celebrated by Iranians and in some countries, which were part of the Persian empire for approximately 2500 years, falls on the day of the March vernal equinox, specifically on March 20 (Borowsk, 1996).
Before Indian independence, scholars had already recognized the inaccuracies in traditional calendars. The calendars were primarily luni-solar, combining lunar months with solar years, but without consistent intercalation, leading to misalignment with the seasons over time. Local *paňcāṅgās* used different methods to calculate celestial events, leading to discrepancies in festival dates. The Indian government Calendar Reform Committee (1952–1955) organized a calendar reform in India, chaired by Dr. Meghnad Saha (noted astrophysicist). The aim was to create a standardized, scientific, and seasonally accurate calendar for official and civil use. They came up with the following recommendations: (i) adopt the Saka Era as the basis for a national calendar, starting from 78 CE, (ii) base the calendar on the tropical year (365.2422 days) to maintain alignment with the seasons, (iii) use solar months rather than lunar months to avoid seasonal drift, (iv) ensure the calendar is in alignment with the Gregorian calendar for administrative efficiency (v) the year shall start on the when the Sun enters *Meśa*, which will be April 14 of the Gregorian Calendar.
Following the Committee’s recommendations, the Government of India officially adopted the Indian National Calendar (Saka calendar) in 1957 for civil purposes. It runs parallel to the Gregorian calendar in all government communications. Calculated using modern astronomical principles from the Indian Ephemeris. The Indian Astronomical Ephemeris is an annual publication by the Positional Astronomy Centre, Kolkata (PAC), providing astronomical data for observational astronomers, *paňcāṅga* makers, and other users (Chatterjee, 2004).

## 2 Analemma and April new year’s day

*Analemma* is a Greek word that represents the Sun’s movement in the sky. It is a visual representation of the Sun’s position in the sky at the same time each day throughout the year, observed from a fixed location on Earth (Zhang, 2021; Jenkins, 2013; Peter, 2012). This pattern forms an elegant figure-eight curve (Fig. 1), with the upper and lower points representing the Northern and Southern solstices as drawn on a globe displayed in Globe Museum, Vienna. The *analemma* curve can be calculated using the following equations (Zhang, 2021).

$$n = -1.5 + 365(Y_{\text{in}} - 2000) + N_{\text{leap}} + \text{Day of the year}$$

$$L = 280.466 + 0.985647n (^{\circ})$$

$$g = 357.528 + 0.985600n (^{\circ})$$

$$\lambda = L + 1.915 \sin g + 0.020 \sin 2g (^{\circ})$$

$$\epsilon = 23.440 - 0.0000004n (^{\circ})$$

$$\alpha = \tan^{-1}(\cos \epsilon \tan \lambda)(180/\pi) (^{\circ})$$

$$\delta = \sin^{-1}(\sin \epsilon \sin \lambda)(180/\pi) (^{\circ})$$

$$R_{\text{ES}} = 1.00014 - 0.01671 \cos g - 0.00014 \cos 2g \text{ (au)}$$

$$E_{\text{min}} = 4(L - \alpha) \text{ min}$$

$$\phi_s = \delta$$

$$\lambda_s = -15(T_{\text{GMT}} - 12 - E_{\text{min}}/60)$$

where: $n$ is the number of days of Terrestrial time from January 1 2000, $Y_{\text{in}}$ is the input year, $N_{\text{leap}}$ is the number of leap years between 2000 and the input year, $L$ is the mean longitude of the Sun, $g$ is the mean anomaly, $\lambda$ is the ecliptic longitude, $\epsilon$ is obliquity of the elliptic, $\alpha$ is the right ascension, $\delta$ is the declination of the Sun, $R_{\text{ES}}$ is the Earth-Sun distance, $E_{\text{min}}$ is the equation of time, $\phi_s$ and $\lambda_s$ are the subsolar point’s coordinates.
In Fig. 2, the calculated *analemma* curve using the above equations is placed on the South Asian map. The intersection occurs at the 9.2°N latitude. The South Indian town of Rameswaram is at 9.24°N latitude and 79.3°E. On this day, parts of Tamil Nadu, Kerala, Sri Lanka, and the town of Surat Thani on the edge of the Gulf of Thailand experience the Sun directly overhead at noon. Notably, the *analemma* figure eight curve intersects at this location around 13–15 April and August 29- September 1. Figure 3 depicts the *analemma* curve for local noon at 9.24°N latitude and 79.3°E longitude utilizing SunEarthTools.[^3]

## 3 Sun path on April 14 at different locations in South Asia

The Suncalc online tool provides the ability to calculate the Sun path in the sky on a specific day at any location on Earth (Botero-Valencia, 2022).[^4] Figures 4, 5, 6 and 7 present the Sun paths for April 14, 2023, at various locations in South India and Sri Lanka. All these locations were chosen strategically for their visibility of the eastern and western horizons, except Madurai. Figure 4 portrays the Sun path at Rameswaram (9.3°N, 79.3°E), where the Sun is directly overhead at noon on April 14, 2023. Figure 5 displays the Sun’s path at Dondra Head or Thevendra Munai (5.93°N, 80.59°E), located at the southern tip of Sri Lanka. The yellow line represents the Sun’s path, which passes north of this location. Figure 6 illustrates the Sun’s path at Kanyakumari or Cape of Comorin (8.08° N, 77.54° E), the southernmost tip of India, where the Sun slightly deviates northward at noon. In Fig. 7, the Sun path at Madurai (9.9° N, 78.1° E) shows a slight southward shift.
As a test on the Suncalc tool, the Sun path was calculated for March 21, 2023, at a point on the equator (0°N, 80° E) (Fig. 8), which shows a straight east-to-west line, indicating that the Sun rises precisely in the east, reaches its zenith at noon, and sets exactly in the west as expected.

## 4 Discussion and conclusion

By using online tools to analyse the Sun’s path and calculate the *analemma’s* figure-eight curve, it becomes clear that the current New Year’s Day, observed between April 13–15, coincides with the intersection point of this celestial pattern. Notably, this is also the day when the Sun is directly overhead at key locations such as Rameswaram in India and Surat Thani in Thailand, underscoring its astronomical significance. Importantly, the date of this *analemma* intersection remains unaffected by the precession of the Earth’s rotational axis, making it an ideal fixed reference point for a solar calendar. Adopting this date as the official solar New Year’s Day could support the Indian government’s efforts to standardize the national calendar by aligning traditional systems based on the *Nirayaṇa* method with the globally used Gregorian calendar.
