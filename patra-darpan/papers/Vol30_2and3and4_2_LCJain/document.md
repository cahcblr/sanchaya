> The Jaina Calendar is considered to be an elaboration of the Vedāṅga Jyotiṣa. It has the same five-year yuga system and is based on the average values of the velocities of the heavenly bodies. The details are available in the *Sūrya Prajñapti*, the *Candra Prajñapti*, and the *Trilokasāra*. The *Tiloyapaṇṇattī* also gives some details in brief. The paper gives certain methods adopted in these texts for finding out the various types of the positions of the sun and the moon in relation to the stars etc. A paper has already been published on the constant-set (*dhruva rāśi*) technique, in this journal.
**Key words:** Jaina Pañcāṅga, Yuga, Avama, Yojana, Gaganakhaṇḍa, Tithi, Muhurta, Paurusi, Yoga.

### INTRODUCTION

The independent astronomical description begins with the *Vedāṅga Jyotiṣa* which must have carried the knowledge of the astronomical *Pūrva aṅga* of the Jainas before Mahāvīra in whose Saṅgha the twelve *aṅgas* were available. The *Vedāṅga Jyotiṣa* was compiled for the *tithi*, *muhūrta*, period, constellation and others. Various dates: 300 B.C., 500 B.C., 1410 B.C., 1338 B.C. were assigned to the work of *Vedāṅga Jyotiṣa* by Maxmüller, Weber, Colebrooke, and Whitney respectively. Tilak fixed it as 1408 B.C. Compiled originally by Ṛṣi Lagadha it contains 36 to 42-44 verses from *Ṛgvedāṅga Jyotiṣa*, 49 verses from *Yajurvedāṅga Jyotiṣa* and 162 verses from the *Atharvavedāṅga Jyotiṣa*. The following five types of principles seem to have been established in the *Vedāṅga Jyotiṣa* which carries several identical constants and methods as in the Jaina calendar, some of which have been placed in the paper.
1. Recognition of a five year *yuga*.
2. Description of the *tithis* and *nakṣatras* etc.
3. Mathematics and prediction as a means.
4. Rules for calculation of astronomical events.
5. The consideration of solstices and equinoxes.

It may be noted that Mahāvīra and his organization under Gaṇadhara Gautama, a Brahmin, specifically mentioned the *Pūrvas* as accepted knowledge of the predecessors, one of them also being the *Karma* theory apart from the astronomy and cosmology. The Digambara Jaina school had some knowledge of the *Pūrvas* contained in the twelfth *aṅga*, and the Śvetāmbara Jaina school claimed to have the knowledge of 11 *aṅgas* only and not the 12th *aṅga*. In all, there were 14 *Pūrvas* whose knowledge persisted upto Bhadrabāhu (c. 4th century B.C.) whose disciple Candragupta Maurya knew 10 *Pūrvas*, as it is said.
The length of a year does not contain an exact number of solar days as also lunar months as integer. The seasons do not begin at a fixed time. The $tith\bar{i}$ is known as the difference between the longitudes of the sun and the moon. When this difference lies between $0^\circ$ and $12^\circ$ the $tith\bar{i}$ is called the *pratipāda*. When the difference lies between $12^\circ$ and $24^\circ$ the $tith\bar{i}$ is called the *dvitīyā*. These are 15 in number and they run from *amāvasyā* to *pūrṇimā* (full moon) or *pūrṇimā* to *amāvasyā* making a cycle. In the Jaina calendar this is given in *gaganakhaṇḍas* (sky-parts). The unit of time is a *ghaṭī* which is $1/10$th part of the 24 hour day. In the Jaina calendar, a day is taken to be of 30 *muhūrtas* and a *muhūrta* is equal to two *nālīs*. In the *Tiloyapaṇṇattī*[^1], $1 \text{ yuga} = 5 \text{ years}$, $1 \text{ year} = 2 \text{ ayana}$, $1 \text{ ayana} = 3 \text{ seasons}$, $1 \text{ season} = 2 \text{ months}$, $1 \text{ month} = 2 \text{ fortnights}$, $1 \text{ fortnight} = 15 \text{ days}$, $1 \text{ day} = 30 \text{ muhūrtas}$, $1 \text{ muhūrta} = 2 \text{ nālīs}$, $1 \text{ nālī} = 38\frac{1}{2} \text{ lavas}$, $1 \text{ lava} = 7 \text{ stokas}$ and $1 \text{ stoka} = 7 \text{ breaths}$ etc.
For civil transaction, the trouble arises from the moment of sunrise which is not fixed and the duration of a $tith\bar{i}$ which is not constant. The reason is that the longitudes of the sun and the moon do not increase at the same rate. As a result the $tith\bar{i}$ discontinues also, or broken, and in two successive days the same $tith\bar{i}$ is also found to occur. In the Jaina calendar the 28 constellations are not having the same span. The ascendant is the point of intersection of the zodiac with the eastern horizon.

## SOME SPECIALITIES OF THE JAINA CALENDAR

The conjuction of the lunar constellation with the solar constellation is settled on the basis of the five year *yuga*. The *yuga* begins with the *abhijit* constellation. The lunar year increases by $6 \times 2$ months than the solar and ends with a month in excess. The lunar year and the solar year become coincident in 30 years or 6 *yugas*.[^2] The solar year, the seasonal (*sāvana*) year, the lunar year, and the constellation (*nakṣatra*) year likewise begin and end on the same day at an interval of 12 *yugas* or 60 years.

### FOUR SAMVATSARAS

Sukumar Ranjana Das has described four types of *saṃvatsara* as follows:[^5]
*Nakṣatra saṃvatsara* $= 12 \text{ nakṣatra-māsa} = 12 \times 27 \frac{21}{67} \text{ days}$
(Sidereal year) $= 327 \frac{51}{67} \text{ days}$
*Yuga saṃvatsara* $= 5 \text{ years}$
There are also *Pramāṇa saṃvatsara* and *Śani saṃvatsara*.
Besides Amolaka Ṛṣi has described one more *saṃvatsara*, the *lakṣaṇa-saṃvatsara*:[^6]
*Nakṣatra saṃvatsara* : This is of 12 types : Śrāvaṇa, Bhādrapada, yāvat Āṣāḍha.
The period taken by the moon to be with the 28 constellations is $27 \frac{21}{67}$ days for a complete revolution. The corresponding year is $27 \frac{21}{67} \times 12 = 327 \frac{51}{67}$ days. The Jupiter completes the revolution round the 28 constellations in a period of $327 \frac{51}{67} \times 12 = 3932 \frac{9}{67}$ days (the *Vṛhaspati yuga*). This great sidereal year begins again on arriving at the *abhijit*.

## MOTION OF PLANETS AND THE TITHIS

The twelve year cycle of *nakṣatra-saṃvatsara* was linked with the sidereal motion of Jupiter. As noted earlier, the sidereal revolution of Jupiter is in $3933 \frac{9}{67}$ days. The modern value is $4332.589$ days which is in excess of $399.455$ days. Thus the beginning of twelve-year cycle of *nakṣatra-saṃvatsara* may not be strictly marked with the beginning of *Abhijit* ($\alpha$-Lyrae) but instead be considered as posited in the zodiacal intercept containing *Śrāvaṇa* ($\alpha$-Aquilae).
Lishk[^11] has marked that

$$744 \text{ Abhivardhana saṃvatsara} = 780 \text{ Solar saṃvatsara}$$

$$= 793 \text{ Ṛtu saṃvatsara}$$

$$= 806 \text{ Candra saṃvatsara}$$

$$= 871 \text{ Nakṣatra saṃvatsara}$$

Hence

$$57 \frac{3}{13} / 12 \text{ Abhivardhana years} = 60/12 \text{ solar years}$$

$$= 61/12 \text{ Ṛtu years}$$

$$= 62/12 \text{ Candra years}$$

$$= 67/12 \text{ Nakṣatra years}$$

From the above relations it is clear that on multiplication of the above by $156$, we get an integral relation between different years implying a bigger cycle of $780$ years or a $13$ times the sixty year cycle.[^11]
Lishk has further discussed the Saturn-year as quoted in the *Sūryaprajñapti* (10.20, Quot. No, 6, 1-8), that the *Mahāgraha Śani* moves among all the *Nakṣatras* in $30$ *saṃvatsaras*.[^12] Thus,

## RELATION BETWEEN SOLSTICES AND THE SHADOW

In the Jaina School of Astronomy, time is measured as a function of shadow-length. Time of the day could be computed by measuring shadow-length of a gnomon. Seasons could be computed or determined through gnomonics. The shadow-length is measured in units of *puruṣas* (height of man), and parts of the day elapsed at various instants. The *Sūryaprajñapti* (ch. 9, v. 9) gives the following data about the relation between the shadow-length and the corresponding part of the day elapsed :

### THE AVAMA (OMITTED LUNAR) NIGHT

There is neither decrease nor increase of time in its eternal nature as it appears in the activities related to the sun etc. Yet the so called *avama*-night, it is in relation to the mutual months. Relative to the work (*karma*) month there happens to be *avama*-night, whereas relative to other month, there is the *ati* (additive) night in the solar month. It was noted by Nemicandra Shastri[^29] that the development of the process about the *avama-tithi* omission was an original or independent contribution of the Jaina School. Similarly the constellational constant-set (*dhruva-rāśi*) might have been responsible in the evolution of the posterior development of the zodiac (12 *rāśis*). Similarly the process of finding out the *nakṣatra* (constellation) relative to the *parva* or *tithi* and its further development is also an original contribution of the Jaina School. He finds the same originality in the process relating to *saṃvatsara*, seasons, and calendrical details.
In a *yuga* the moon moves through 67 constellations, hence there are $67 \times 6 = 402$ seasons in a *yuga*. In the calculations about the seasons three types of constant-sets are used :
(1) 61305, (2) 9455, (3) 18910.
During an event with the constellation, the moon has six seasons, because in a *yuga* there are 67 such constellational events. In order to find out the days of a season, it is known that the constellational event with the moon is of $27 + 19/62 + 29/(62 \times 67)$ days (*ahorātras*). On dividing this amount by 6 we get the number of days as $4 + 34/62 + 16/(62 \times 67)$ when a moon's season is complete. During this period the first half season remains in the previous *yuga* and the second half prevails from the beginning of the *yuga*.

### THE PHASES OF THE MOON

The *tithi* of the moon month is $29 \frac{32}{62}$ *muhūrtas*. On multiplying this by 30 *tithis*, the moon's month appears as of $885 \frac{30}{62}$ *muhūrtas*. When this is divided by 2 we get the $442 \frac{46}{62}$ *muhūrtas* as the duration of each of the dark and white half consequent upon motion of the day ascending node (*dina-Rāhu*) which gives a simile of the darkness and the illumination. On dividing $442 \frac{46}{62}$ by 30 we get $14 \frac{47}{62}$ days. During this measure of days the plane of the moon gets on being covered by the *Rāhu*. On the first *tithi* the first part of the moon's celestial plane gets covered by *Rāhu*. After that it begins to get uncovered. The moon's celestial plane is in 63 parts, hence in every orbit its 1/4th part or four parts every day get covered. By the 15th *tithi* 60 parts are covered. Two parts remain uncovered. In this way the *Sūryaprajñapti* describes the motion, coverage etc. about the *Dina-rāhu* in various orbits[^30].
