# THE CONCEPT OF CYCLIC QUADRILATERALS : ITS ORIGIN AND DEVELOPMENT IN INDIA (FROM THE AGE OF ŚULBA SŪTRAS TO BHĀSKARA I)

A. MUKHOPADHYAY AND M.R. ADHIKARI[^1]
*(Received 12 December 1995; after revision 29 November, 1996)*
In the ‘Polygonal Approximation to Circle' due to Mādhāvacārya (1340-1425 AD), the process of approximation started with circumscribing a given circle by a square without recourse to the idea of a square inscribed in a circle, or more generally, to the idea of a cyclic quadrilateral, erstwhile very much familiar to all the Indian scholars of mathematics. In this paper, our aim is to study the development of the concept of cyclic quadrilateral in India from the age of Śulba Sūtras (800-500 BC)² to the time of Bhāskara I (around 574 AD)³ with emphasis on the genesis of cyclic quadrilaterals having all its sides unequal.
**Key Words :** Āpastamba, Vahirvṛtta, Avāntaradikṣu, Baudhāyana. Bhāskara I, Gārhapatya, Konacatuṣṭayasprgvṛttam, Parimaṇḍalāyām, Śulba Sūtra.
A 'cyclic quadrilateral' basically means a quadrilateral with its vertices on the circumference of a circle. The word ‘cyclic’ here is far from its usual meaning of ‘occurrence or movement in cycles’⁴. Euclid’s Elements⁵ used ‘a quadrilateral inscribed in a circle’ or instead of using the adjective ‘cyclic’ to qualify the word ‘quadrilateral’. Śulba sūtras' straightforward mention of constructing a square as large as possible within a given circle (*parimaṇḍalāyām yāvatsambhavettāvat samacaturaśram*⁶) did not furnish any nomenclature either for the circumscribing circle or for the rectilinear figure circumscribed. The term *vahirvṛtta* for the circle circumscribing a rectilinear figure (triangle or quadrilateral) was common to Brahmagupta⁷ (628 AD⁸), Mahāvīrācārya⁹ (850 AD¹⁰), Śrīpati¹¹ (1039 AD¹²). Parameśvara¹³ (1360-1455 AD¹⁴) used the phrase ‘*Konacatuṣṭayasprg vṛttam*’ (a circle in contact with the four angles) in the sense perfectly in conformity with the relevant Euclidean concept¹⁵ and bearing a close resemblance to the corresponding Brahmagupta-terminology. Śaṅkara Pārāśava (1500-1560 AD¹⁶) was the first Indian scholar to prescribe the concise nomenclature ‘*vṛttagata caturaśram*’¹⁷, the adjective ‘*vṛttagata*’ being more meaningful than the corresponding word ‘Cyclic’.
The evolution of concept of cyclic quadrilateral in India from the particular

## The concept of cyclic quadrilateral in the śulba age

The first available definite manifestation of the concept during this age (800-500 BC) is in the form of the then idea of a square inscribed in a circle. The circular *Gārhapatya* altar mentioned in *Baudhāyana Śulba-sūtra* (S-1)[^2] and described in *Āpastamba Śulba-sūtra* (S-2) or the *Rathacakra Citi* (chariot-wheel alter) described by *Baudhāyana* (S-3 to S-7) and also by *Āpastamba* (S-8, S-9) essentially involves the construction of a square as large (in area) as possible (*yāvatsambhavettāvat*) within a circle of a given diameter. That the corners of the square lie on the circumference of the circle has been made clear by Baudhāyana in the line : ‘*pradhyanīkeṣu sraktayo bhavanti*’ (S-10).
(*pradhyanīkeṣu = pradhi + anīkeṣu; pradhi* means the felly or the rim of a wheel, *anīka* means the edge and *srakti* means the corner. *Sraktayaḥ* is the plural of *srakti*).
The method of inscribing a square within a circle of a given diameter in connection with the construction of *Gārhapatya* altar has been outlined in the *Āpastamba Śulba-sūtras* (S-2, S-11) thus :
‘Fixing a gnomon (or a stake) in the middle of the earthen foundation (laid on the ground), a circle is drawn with half *vyāyāma* (approximately 3 ft) as radius’. ‘On (the circular contour of) the base, the points in the intermediate positions (*avāntaradiaḥ*) of the compass are taken to mark the corners of the inscribed square.’
Kapardisvāmī (a few centuries before 11th century AD²⁰), a commentator of *Āpastamba Śulba-sūtra* makes the matter more explicit thus (S-12):
---

‘There, on the earthen foundation (*mṛdo vṛttikāyāḥ*), this circle (drawn with $1/2 \ vy\bar{a}y\bar{m}a$ as the radius) is considered (*parāmṛśyate*). With this circle, a square as extended as possible is drawn’.
‘The circumference is divided into four (equal) segments (with the help of the lines seeking East-West and North-South directions). Marking the intermediate directions (on the circumference), the points obtained (on the circumference) are the corners of the (required) square. Four equal arcs (*pradhikas*) lie in the four directions (North, South, East, West) outside the square’.
The length of a side of this square inscribed in the circle of diameter one $vy\bar{a}y\bar{a}ma$ (i.e., radius $r = 1/2 \ vy\bar{a}y\bar{a}ma$, where $1 \ vy\bar{a}y\bar{a}ma = 96 \ aṅgulas$, $1 \ aṅgula = 34 \ tila$), as has been calculated by Kapardisvāmī is sixty eight aṅgulas less four tila (S-13). This tallies with the modern calculation that a side of the inscribed square $= r\sqrt{2}$ as, $r\sqrt{2} = 67.882251 \ aṅgulas$ (taking $\sqrt{2} = 1.4142136$), which is equal to $68 \ aṅgulas - 4.0034662 \ tila \sim 68 \ aṅgulas - 4 \ tila$. The same result has been given by Dvārakānātha Yajvān, a commentator of *Baudhāyana Śulba sūtra*²¹ in the line:
‘*Viṣkambhārdhadvikarṇyāṣṭaṣaṣtyāṅgulena catustilonena samacaturaśram*’ (where ‘*viṣkambhārdhadvikaraṇī*’ means the radius multiplied by $\sqrt{2}$).
The concept of cyclic quadrilaterals during this period was limited within the scope of a square inscribed in a circle with unambiguous mention of its cyclic nature. Rectangles (*dīrghacaturaśram*) and isosceles trapeziums (*caturaśramekato’nimat animatpārśvaḥ*)²², whose methods of construction and of quadrature Indian scholars were conversant with since the time of *Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa* (2000 BC)²³, remained unidentified as cyclic quadrilaterals in the Śulba period. A rectangle was probably identified as a cyclic quadrilateral in a later period of Jaina culture. This limitation in the concept prevailed even in the early medieval period (400-600 AD). In this period, Geometry, which was erstwhile a part of *Kalpasūtra* came to be regarded as an integral part of ‘*Gaṇita*’ and Indian scholars mainly focussed their attention on the study and development of Trigonometry within the scope of *Jyotiṣa*²⁴.

## Āryabhaṭa I passed over the study of cyclic quadrilateral :

Āryabhaṭa I (born 475 AD) held that a quadrilateral can be constructed by its diagonals (S-14). Bhāskara I (around 574 AD) in his commentary on *Āryabhaṭīya*²⁵ explained this in the following way (S-15) :
---
56
‘A thread equal in length to the given diagonal is to be streched obliquely’.
‘Through the midpoint (of this thread), the second (thread equal in length to the second diagonal) is also to be streched obliquely (being itself bisected at the midpoint of the first one) in the form of a ‘*Svastika*’.
‘By filling up the gaps in the sides, i.e., by joining the endpoints of the diagonals (*etayoḥ pārśvāni pūritāni*), the quadrilateral is completed (*caturaśrakṣetram niṣpannam*).
According to Monier Monier Williams²⁶, ‘*Svastika*’ is, in general, a symbol consisting of four equal line-segments (spokes of a wheel) crossing each other at right angles. If this meaning of *svastika* be accepted, Āryabhaṭa I’s above method for construction of a quadrilateral refers to only a square or a rhombus. If, however, the word be taken for a pair of line-segments bisecting each other, then the resulting figure (though not uniquely determined by the diagonals only) will refer to a parallelogram or a rectangle.
However, no specific study of cyclic quadrilaterals, in general, can be witnessed in Āryabhaṭa I’s work, though his acquaintance with regular polygons (inscribed in a circle) was quite probable²⁷.
Varāhamihira’s (505 AD) *Pañcasiddhāntikā*²⁸ also does not throw light on the said field of study.

## Bhāskara I's contribution to the study of cyclic quadrilateral :

In the course of discussions on the verse 10 (Appendix) in the chapter ‘*Gaṇitapāda*’ of *Āryabhaṭīya*, Bhāskara I referring to the prākṛt Gāthā (of Jaina origin) : ‘*Vikkhambhavaggadasaguṇakaraṇī vaṭṭassa parirāyo hodi*’ (S-16) put forward one particular rectangle (S-17) and expressed his idea on how this rectangle can be treated as a cyclic quadrilateral. Here Bhāskara depicted that ‘taking a rectangle one unit wide and three units long (*ekatrivistarāyāmāyata caturaśram*), if a circle be drawn with the diagonal (of the rectangle) $\sqrt{10}$ unit long as the diameter (*Karṇena daśakaranikenaiva tadviṣkambha.......*), then the circle can be judged (*tatpramāṇobhavatīti*) as that circumscribing (*paridhirveṣṭyamānaḥ*) (the rectangle); that (the area of the circle) also is calculable (*tadapisādhyameva*).
The identification of a rectangle as a cyclic quadrilateral has been made clear in the remark that within the circle (circumscribing the rectangle), there are four segments of the circle (*vṛttakṣetre catvāri dhanukṣetrāṇi*) surrounding

the rectangle (S-18).
Save this particular case Bhāskara I did not turn towards discussion of any other type of quadrilateral, which he himself identified as cyclic.
Bhāskara I was the first to open discussion on quadrilaterals with all the four sides unequal and none of the opposite sides parallel. He set the following epoch-making example (S-19) :
‘The face (of length) $5^2$ (units) joined to the base (of length) 60 (units) by the flanks (of length) $13 \times 3$ (units) and $13 \times 4$ (units) in order, forms a quadrilateral with unequal sides having perpendiculars (from the ends of the face to the base) unequal (*asyayāvavalambakau tau na sadṛśau*)’.
Without making any comment on the speciality of this quadrilateral, he just remarked, ‘Here whatever (quadrilateral) has been brought under study, has the perpendiculars from the ends of the face to the base equal (*atra ca yadupadiśyate tasya yāvavalambakau tau tulya saṅkhyau*) with the note that for a quadrilateral with all the four sides unequal (as considered in the example), the perpendiculars (from the ends of the face to the base) being indeterminate (*durjñātatvādavalambakasya*), only the area is the subject under consideration (S-20).
The novelty of the art of forming the *viṣama* quadrilateral (in the example), though not mentioned by Bhāskara I, will be evident from the following discussion :
If two right-angled triangles with sides 3 unit, 4 unit, 5 unit, and 5 unit, 12 unit, 13 unit be considered, then multiplying the base and the upright of one separately by the hypotenuse of the other, the four products :
$3 \times 13$ unit, $4 \times 13$ unit, $5 \times 5$ unit, $5 \times 12$ unit, i.e., $13 \times 3$ unit, $13 \times 4$ unit, $5^2$ unit and 60 unit are the four sides of the quadrilateral mentioned in the example.
Bhāskara pointed out that 60 unit being taken for the length of the base, the face will be $5^2$ unit long while the flanks will be $13 \times 3$ unit and $13 \times 4$ unit.
This art of forming a *viṣama* quadrilateral with the help of two preassumed right-angled triangles was later expressed in the most generalised way by Brahmagupta.

in the form of a verse (S-21) in *Gaṇitādhyāyaḥ* in *Brāhmasphuṭasiddhānta* (written in 628 AD). The verse states :
‘Of two right-angled triangles, the base and the upright of one are multiplied separately by the hypotenuse of the other. The four products (so obtained) are the four sides (of a *viṣama* quadrilateral), the largest of which is the base, the smallest is the face and the remaining two are the two flanks’.
Expressing mathematically, if (a, b, c) and (x, y, z) be the two triads representing the sides of two right-angled triangles with $c^2 = a^2 + b^2$ and $z^2 = x^2 + y^2$, then the four products $cx, cy, az, bz$ will give the sides of a *viṣama* quadrilateral, the largest and the smallest of the four products will be the base and the face respectively.
The same rule or modifications thereof, but differing in the mode of delivery, has been given by :
*Mahāvīrācārya* (middle of 9th century AD) (S-22)
*Bhāskara II*[^29] (1114 AD) (S-23)
*Nārāyaṇa Paṇḍita*[^30] (1356 AD) (S-24)
The modification, as advanced by *Mahāvīrācārya* and by *Nārāyaṇa Paṇḍita*, involves multiplication of the four sides of the *viṣama* quadrilateral obtained following the above Brahmagupta Rule by the smaller hypotenuse. Each of the above mentioned three scholars accepted *Bhāskara I*’s aforementioned example as the key example of a *viṣama* quadrilateral.
Brahmagupta pointed out that the circumradius of a *viṣama* quadrilateral (generated in accordance with the above rule) is half the square-root of the sum of squares of a pair of opposite sides (S-25). Though this rule is sufficient to identify *Bhāskara I*’s particular *viṣama caturaśra* as a cyclic quadrilateral, still *Brāhmasphuṭasiddhānta* does not accommodate *Bhāskara I*’s example, and therefore, there is no categorical mention of the cyclic nature of the particular *viṣama* quadrilateral of the said example. *Mahāvīrācārya*, on the other hand, in the chapter ‘*Janya Vyāvahāraḥ*’ in his *Gaṇitasāra-saṃgrahaḥ*, not only put forward the rule (S-26) for the circum-diameter of the five types (S-27) of quadrilateral, namely : (1) square (*samacaturaśra*), (2) rectangle or equidichastic quadrilateral (*dvidvisamacaturaśra*), (3) isosceles trapezium or equilateral quadrilateral (*dvisama caturaśra*), (4) trapezium with three sides equal (*trisamacaturaśra*) and (5) *viṣama caturaśra* (inequilateral quadrilateral, i.e., quadrilateral with unequal sides generated with the help of two right triangles), but also was the first to express his

## CONCLUSIONS

1. The method of circumscribing a given square following the determination of its circum-centre and circum-diameter owes its origin to the *Śulba* Age (800-500 BC) at the least, that is, much before the Euclidean pedagogy ushered in the field of Geometry. That a quadrilateral is defined to be inscribed in a circle if its vertices lie on the circumference of the circle is also a contribution of this age, though the idea was limited within the scope of a square.
2. The study of a rectangle as a cyclic quadrilateral—a significant evidence of ancient Jaina culture in India motivated *Bhāskara I* to consider the quadrature problem of a circle in a way different from the customary process of exhaustion[^32]. He pointed out that a circle circumscribing a rectangle consists of four circular segments and one rectangle, the union of areas of all of which should be the area of the circumscribing circle. But in practice, the calculated areas of the components, when added, do not give the area of the circle (S-28). This indicates *Bhāskara I*’s contention over the calculability of the area of a circle with the help of the then formula involving approximations to $\pi$ in use in his times.
3. The art of forming a *viṣama* quadrilateral (later found to be cyclic), as was hinted at by *Bhāskara I* in his example (S-19), demands special credit as it opened a new channel for furthering the study of cyclic quadrilaterals in India in a direction unknown to the followers of Euclidean thoughts.
