# INTERCALATION IN THE VEDIC TEXTS

**PRABHAKAR GONDHALEKAR***
(Received 15 February 2008; revised 3 September 2008)
The intercalation schemes that might have been used by the Āryas have been identified from an analysis of the Vedic texts (the *Saṃhitās*, the *Brāhmaṇas* and the *Sūtras*). It is shown that during the Vedic period a number of intercalation schemes may have been in use. A ‘perfect’ scheme for synchronising the tropical year and the Vedic ritual year has also been identified in these texts. The Āryas were far more creative in matching their sacrificial sessions to the cycle of seasons then has been assumed in the past. The algorithm for intercalation in *Vedāṅga Jyotiṣa* and the origins of a number of features of the calendar of *Vedāṅga Jyotiṣa* can be traced back to the Vedic texts.
**Key words:** *Gavām ayana*, Intercalation, Metonic cycle, Ritual year, Sidereal year, Synodic year, *Vedāṅga Jyotiṣa* calendar

## 1. INTRODUCTION

*Vedāṅga Jyotiṣa*, one of the six disciplines (*aṅgas*/limbs) studied by Vedic priests, provided the priests with the means to compute the prescribed times for performing sacrifices. It is one of the two Vedic texts that is not devoted to the description and exposition of the rituals. The date of composition of this text is not known; astronomical data in the text suggest composition sometime between 1370 BC and 1150 BC[^1] but a later date of approximately 500 BC has also been suggested[^2]. This is the only text of astronomy available from the Vedic period and it is likely to be the culmination of a process of identification and classification of calendric concepts that must have started at a much earlier age. This earlier period can be explored with the aid of Vedic texts (i.e. the *Saṃhitās*, the *Brāhmaṇas* and the *Sūtras*); these texts are devoted to the description, exposition and justification

The date of composition of these texts is also not known; the oldest text, *Ṛgveda Saṃhitā*, is believed to have been composed between 1700 BC and 1200 BC[^3] and the consensus of opinion is that most if not all of the other texts were composed before 500 BC. From these texts following elements of a calendar can be identified;
*   The seasons – in *Ṛgveda Saṃhitās* ($RV$) three, five and six seasons have been mentioned. In the post-$Ṛgvedic$ texts there are five or six (and rather infrequently, seven) seasons in a year.
*   The tropical or the seasonal year – this can be inferred from a number of verses in the *Ṛgveda Saṃhitās* and in other Vedic texts, in which the age of a person etc. is given by the number of elapsed seasons (usually autumn/*śarad*) e.g. $RV$. X.85.39[^4].
*   The synodic year – this can be inferred from verses like "the moon is the one that shapes the years" e.g. $RV$. X.85.5[^5].
*   The month – the canonical length of a Vedic month was 30 days, this is the rounded-up mean synodic month of 29.53 days; the moon is often referred to as *māsakṛt* (month maker) also the word *māsa* means both the moon and the month. In the post-$Ṛgvedic$ texts each season was assigned two months, sometimes two seasons were combined.
*   A ritual year – the length of this year was 360 days (i.e. 12 months of 30 days each) e.g. $RV$. I.164.11[^6].
*   An intercalary month – a thirteenth month (*adhimāsa*) is often mentioned and the phrase "with the thirteenth month you obtain the year" occurs frequent in the Vedic texts. The earliest reference to a thirteenth month is in $RV$. I.164.15[^7].
*   A period of intercalation – in the post-$Ṛgvedic$ texts a group of five years is frequently mentioned but (with one exception, as discussed below) no specific role is identified for this group. In the *Vedāṅga Jyotiṣa* a five year period of intercalation is used.
It is obvious that both the synodic year (of 354.36 days) and the ritual year (of 360 days) will lag behind the tropical (seasonal) year (of 365.24 days) after the fiducial year and a scheme of intercalation would have been essential for the Vedic priests to perform their rituals and ceremonies at the

The Vedic priests were also aware that the tropical/seasonal year was at least 365 days long as they point out in *Taittirīya Saṃhitā* ($TS$. This need to correct the ritual year was recognised in the oldest Vedic texts available to us; in $RV$. I.25.8. it is noted that "*Dhṛtavrata* (*Varuṇa*) has knowledge of the productive twelve months, he knows, that which is extra". The nature of "that which is extra" is not stated but the productive twelve months refers to a ritual year of twelve months (each of thirty days) and it is reasonable to assume that the "extra" refers to the days required to match the length (in days) of ritual year and the tropical year. The addition of extra days (five) to a ritual year of 360 days was practised by ancient Iranians and is mentioned in Avestan[^10]. Similarly, for many centuries ancient Egyptian priests added extra days to their ritual year to bring it closer in length to the tropical year.
Apart from the few statements like those quoted above, the Āryas have left us no clues to reconstruct the procedure(s) they may have used to synchronise the tropical year and the ritual year. Perhaps the first (in the South Asian cultural sphere) reference to intercalation is in *Vedāṅga Jyotiṣa*. This has encouraged the belief that "no systematic intercalation scheme can be attributed to this (Vedic) period"[^11]. And yet the Āryas performed their rites and ceremonies, both simple and intricate, over hundreds of years. The Vedic texts give times when some of these rituals should be performed and a sacrificer is constantly urged (and warned) that the full efficacy of a ritual is only accrued by following the procedures of a ritual ‘to the latter’. The ceremonies like *Darśapūrṇamāsa* (new and full moon) and *Cāturmāsya* (four monthly or the three seasons) would have required the ability to forecast and identify appropriate days for performance of ceremonies. It is inconceivable that these ceremonies and rituals would not have been performed at the appointed and proper times. A closer look at the Vedic texts indicates that the Āryas were aware of problems encountered in getting the time right for their rituals. For example, *Aitareya Brāhmaṇa* ($AB$. VII. 11; XXXII. 10) and *Kauṣītaki Brāhmaṇa* ($KB$. III.1)[^12] describe ceremonies to be performed at new-moon. But in *Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa* ($SB$.XI.1.5.1)[^13] the sacrificer is

Starting with the nineteenth century pioneer Weber[^15,16], the Vedic texts have been studied and analysed (to various depths) by Thibaut[^17,18], Tilak[^19] and Dikshit[^20] to identify the calendar of this period. ## 2. INTERCALATION

A number of early civilizations timed their ritual and social ceremonies by the synodic month. It was also recognised that a synodic year of 12 months and about 354 days did not ‘keep pace’ with the tropical year of about 365 days. In the Middle East the attempts to synchronise the synodic and the tropical years can be traced back to about eighth century BC[^32], but the best known early attempt to synchronise the synodic calendar and the tropical calendar is the Metonic cycle. This was devised (more likely adopted from the Babylonians) around 432 BC by the astronomer Meton of Athens. This scheme is based on the observation (known to the Babylonians) that after 19 years (235 lunations or synodic months) the moon’s phase recurs on the same day of the tropical year. The greatest advantage of the Metonic cycle was that it offered a lunar calendar with a definite rule for inserting intercalary months to keep in step with the cycle of seasons. It also gave an accurate average length of the tropical year: 365.25 days. The scheme was so successful that it formed the basis of the calendar adopted in the Seleucid Empire (Mesopotamia) and was used in the Jewish calendar and the calendar of the Christian Church.
It is not known if the Āryas also had developed an arithmetic procedure for synchronising their ritual year and the tropical year. As mentioned above, the thirteenth month can be traced back to *Ṛgveda Saṃhitā* and a thirteenth month is mentioned, either explicitly or implicitly, in all post-*Ṛgvedic Saṃhitās* and the *Brāhmaṇas*. Unfortunately the Āryas have not left us the algorithm they might have used for intercalation. It has been assumed that the Āryas would have followed the arithmetic approach typified by Meton and other ancient Greek calendar makers. In the simplest application of such a scheme a month of 30 days could be intercalated after ‘$n$’ years to synchronise the tropical year of 365.24 days and the ritual year of 360 days, that is;

$$365.24 \times n = 360.0 \times n + 30$$

$$n = 5.73$$

The tropical and the ritual years would be perfectly synchronised if a thirty day month is intercalated after five years and (about) nine months. The Vedic texts have no references to this form of intercalation. But a group of five years is frequently mentioned, in calendrical context, in the post-*Ṛgvedic Saṃhitās* and the *Brāhmaṇas*. In the *Vedāṅga Jyotiṣa* a group of five years is called a *yuga* and is the period of intercalation (see below). Guided by *Vedāṅga Jyotiṣa* rather than by the Vedic texts, it has been assumed that the Āryas may have intercalated a thirteenth month every fifth year. That is;

$$Tropical\ year;\ 5 \times 365.24 = 1826.20$$

$$Ritual\ year;\ 5 \times 360 + 30 = 1830.00$$

$$Difference\ (Tropical - Ritual)\ year = -3.8\ days$$

This scheme of intercalation results in an average year of 366 days. In this scheme, after five years, the tropical year would have lagged behind the ritual year by 3.8 days and this would accumulate to thirty days after about thirty-nine years and six months. The ‘shift in the seasons’ would have been noticed long before thirty-nine years. The annual difference between the accumulated number of days in the tropical years and in the ritual years, over five hundred years, is shown in Fig. 2. The difference between the intercalation scheme of the Metonic cycle and the ‘five-year-*yuga*’ cycle is dramatic and it should be obvious that the Āryas could not have followed this rather crude scheme of intercalation.

The Āryas could have used a ‘six-year-*yuga*’ for intercalation i.e. added a (thirteenth) month of 30 days in every sixth year. The only reason for considering this scheme is a reference, in calendrical context, to a group of six year in *Taittirīya Brāhmaṇas* (TB.III.10.4).[^33] That is;

$$6 \times 365.24 = 2191.44\ days$$

$$6 \times 360 + 30 = 2190.00\ days$$

$$Difference\ (Tropical - Ritual)\ year = 1.44\ days$$

After six years the tropical year would have gained 1.44 days on the ritual year and this would have accumulated to thirty days after one hundred and twenty five years. This scheme of intercalation results in an average year of 365 days. This scheme is far more stable than the ‘five-year-*yuga*’ scheme nevertheless it is not particularly satisfactory over long periods. Two possible schemes of intercalation after six years are suggested in *SB*. In *SB*. IX 1.1.43. and *SB*. IX 3.3.18. a thirteenth month of thirty-five days is suggested and in *SB*. X. 4.3.8. *SB*. X. 4.3.19. and *SB*. X 5.4.5., a thirteenth month of thirty-six days is suggested. The reasoning behind these two schemes could be as follows;

$$7 \times 365.24 = 2556.68\ days$$

$$6 \times 360 + 360 + 35 = 2555.00\ days$$

$$6 \times 360 + 360 + 36 = 2556.00\ days$$

The result of intercalating a thirteenth month of thirty-five days in the seventh year is very similar to that of intercalating a thirteenth month of thirty days in the sixth year, that is, the difference (in the number of days in the tropical year and the ritual year) of 1.68 days will accumulate to thirty days after one hundred and twenty five years and the average length of the year would be 365 days. But in the scheme of intercalating a thirteenth month of thirty-six days in the seventh year the difference (in the number of days in the tropical year and the ritual year) of 0.68 days will accumulate to thirty days after three hundred and eight years and ten months and the average length of the year would be 365.14 days. The intercalation of thirty-five and thirty-six days were identified by Weber[^34] but he erroneously assumed an intercalation period of six years.
It is possible to make a very simple amendment to the ‘five-year-*yuga*’ scheme of intercalation to obtain a very satisfactory synchronisation of the tropical year and the Vedic ritual year. This is achieved by omitting the intercalary thirteenth month (of 30 days) in every eighth *yuga* i.e. every forty years.[^35] The results of this scheme of intercalation over five-hundred years are shown in Fig. 2. and it is obvious that this is an exceptionally stable scheme. And yet there is no evidence in the Vedic texts that the Āryas followed this scheme of intercalation. This cannot have been due to their lack of ability to perform simple arithmetic operations because in the construction of their ritual fire-altars, the Āryas demonstrate a high degree of mathematical skill.
The only Vedic text that describes an intercalation scheme is *Maitrāyaṇīya Saṃhitā* (MS.I.10.8).[^36] In this text two type of ritualists can be identified; the $ṛtu-yājins$ and the $cāturmāsya-yājins$. The $ṛtu-yājins$ celebrated the three seasonal sacrifices ($cāturmāsya$) on the first full-moon day that followed the beginning of spring, summer and autumn. These *yajins* were not concerned with the number of days between the seasons or the length of the lunation but relied entirely on observations to perform the required rituals. The $cāturmāsya-yājins$, on the other hand, performs the seasonal sacrifices at fixed number of lunations. But these *yajins* had to follow (arithmetic) rules to keep in step with the seasons. The scheme suggested is as follows: the *yajins* celebrated the $cāturmāsya$ ceremonies for three years (of thirty-six synodic months) at fixed intervals and followed this by an intercalated thirteenth month, the ceremonies were then performed for further two years (of twenty-four synodic months) followed by another intercalated thirteenth month. Both intercalated months were of thirty days. A possible scheme of alternate lunations of thirty and twenty-nine days respectively is as follows;

$$18 \times 30 + 18 \times 29 + 30 = 1092\ days$$

$$12 \times 30 + 12 \times 29 + 30 = 738\ days$$

$$Total = 1830.00\ days$$

$$Tropical\ year:\ 365.24 \times 5 = 1826.20\ days$$

This scheme can be improved slightly (that is, reduce the difference between the number of days in five tropical years and the number of days in five intercalated years) by altering the number of months with lunations of thirty and twenty-nine days. But a (near) perfect match is not possible. As discussed above, in this scheme the ritual year (of the $cāturmāsya-yājins$) would have gained on the tropical year and would have required additional correction. MS.I.10.8. is the only Vedic text that has unambiguously identified the purpose of grouping five years in a calendar but in this *Saṃhitā* this five year period is not called a ‘*yuga*’. This *Saṃhitā* has not told us why a period of five year was used for intercalation, for this we have to turn elsewhere.
The daily observances of the *sattra* of *Gavām ayana* follow the apparent annual motion of the sun. This *sattra* has two foci, the *Viṣuvant* and *Mahāvrata* days and these correspond respectively to the summer and the winter solstice as explained in *KB*. XIX.3.[^37] The day of the start of the *sattra* is not given in *KB*. XIX.3., but *AB*. IV.26; XIX.4) prescribe that the *sattra* should commence in the cool season ($śiśira$ in the months of $māgha$ or $phālguna$), this passage also enjoins against starting the *sattra* in any other season. More definite information on the day of the start of the *sattra* is given in *TS*.VII.4.8.[^38] and with minor variations in the wording and with additional information, in *Pañcaviṃśa Brāhmaṇa* (PB.V.9).[^39] The importance of these passages has been recognised for a long time and these passages have been discussed extensively by *mīmāṃsakas* and Sāyaṇa (fourteenth century, died about 1387). In the nineteenth century the passages again came under scrutiny, this time of Weber[^40] and Tilak.[^41] More recently the passage has been discussed by Chakravarty.[^42] The *mīmāṃsakas* attempted to interpret the passages and understand their implications and these have been used by the nineteenth and twentieth century scholars to discuss the New Year Day of the Vedic calendar. From these texts no single chronological marker can be identified for the start of the ritual year. According to *KB*. XIX.3. “on the new moon of $māgha$ he (sun) rests, being about to turn north....” and the *Mahāvrata* would have been performed on this day and the ceremony concluding *Gavām ayana* (Udayanīya Atirātra) would have been performed on the following day. Thus the new ritual year must have started (soon) after the winter solstice and this is in agreement with *AB*. IV.26; XIX.4. It is however unlikely that the new cycle of the *sattra* was started two days after the *Mahāvrata* as the start of the Vedic ceremonies was usually on either the day after the new moon or on the full moon day. It is also impossible for the winter solstice to coincide with a new moon (of any month) every year and the requirements of *KB*. XIX.3. are unrealistic. The conclusion of the discussion of the *mīmāṃsakas*[^43] is that the start of the *sattra* was on the full moon following the *ekāṣṭakā* immediately after the winter solstice. This would be the full moon of the month of $māgha$. Śabara[^44] (approximately 1 BC) points out that “every eighth day of every month is *ekāṣṭakā*” (*sūtra* 33). The (Vedic) month starts on the day after the new-moon, (that is why the waxing phase is called *purvapakṣa*), thus according to Śabara, *ekāṣṭakā* would be the first quarter. The stability of this calendar can be tested; the essential steps are as follows;
*   Find the date of the winter solstice; this is the end of the tropical year and the end of the *sattra* and the next day is the start of the new tropical year.
*   Find the date of the following first quarter.
*   Find the date of the following full-moon, the new *sattra* starts on this day.
The date of the winter solstice is determined by computing the minimum value (around -23.5°) of the declination of the sun. This is followed by computing the angle of separation between the sun and the moon, the date of the first quarter is when this angle is 90°. Only the date that is after the winter solstice is retained. The date of the following full-moon is when the angle of separation between the sun and the moon is 180°. The length of the tropical year is the separation (in days) between two consecutive dates of the winter solstice. The start of two consecutive *sattras* is separated by the number of days between the two full-moon days determined by the above procedure. This is the ritual or *sattra* year that is, a synodic year corrected by intercalation. A sample of data obtained is given in Table 1.

These passages from the Vedic texts do not have a direct bearing on the present scheme but they demonstrate that the Vedic ritualists were familiar with years of different lengths and the need to adjust the length of their ritual year. Within a *sattra*, different groups of ritualists may have followed their own particular ways of timing the start (or the end) of rituals and ceremonies, as was done by the *ṛtu-yājins* and *cāturmāsya-yājins* of *MS*.I.10.8.

The author of the *Yajurveda* recension is not known. In the following discussion all references to *Vedāṅga Jyotiṣa* are to the translation and interpretation of Kuppanna Sastry[^49].
The intercalation period of the calendar of *Vedāṅga Jyotiṣa* is (approximately) five tropical years and it is called "*yuga*". The verses *RJ.* 5-6 (i.e. the $5^{\text{th}}$ and $6^{\text{th}}$ verse of *Arca Jyotiṣa*) and *YJ.* 6-7 (i.e. $6^{\text{th}}$ and $7^{\text{th}}$ verse of *Yājuṣa-Jyotiṣa*) state that "When the sun and the moon occupy the same region of the zodiac (sky) together with the asterism of *Śraviṣṭhā*, at that time begins the *yuga*, the (synodic) month of *māgha*, the (solar seasonal) month of *tapas* etc., .........the sun and the moon begin to move north.........". That is, the intercalation period starts when the star (or star group) *Śraviṣṭhā* rises helically at winter solstice. Apart from identifying the epoch (via the helical rising of *Śraviṣṭhā*) when this scheme of intercalation was formulated or more likely formalized, the start of a *yuga* in the *Jyotiṣa* is same as that inferred from *TS.VII.4.8*. Also the season and the month at the start of the year are same as those prescribed in *AB. IV.26; (XIX.4)*. But the composer(s) of the *Vedāṅga Jyotiṣa* cannot have meant the start of the month *māgha* or the season *tapas* every year as the Vedic month starts the day after a new moon and it is impossible to have a new moon at winter solstice every year. This anomaly is also similar to that encountered in the calendar based on *TS.VII.4.8*. A *yuga* is $1830$ *sāvana* (civil) days or the mean length of a *sāvana* (tropical) years is $366$ *sāvana* days (*YJ. 28*). The length of $1830$ *sāvana* days of a *yuga* is fundamental to *Vedāṅga Jyotiṣa*; almost all parameters and algorithms of the *Vedāṅga Jyotiṣa* are based on this number. This is not a naturally occurring number like a month or a year. It seems improbable that the author(s) of the *Vedāṅga Jyotiṣa* simply made-up this number for their intercalation scheme. As shown above this period of intercalation follows from the mathematical interpretation of *TS.VII.4.8* (and *PB.V.9.*). It is possible that the author(s) of the *Vedāṅga Jyotiṣa* were familiar with the scheme of *TS.VII.4.8* that required observations of the solstice and

the phases of the moon and reduced it to an arithmetic rule based on an intercalation period of $1830$ *sāvana* days. The *Vedāṅga Jyotiṣa* notes that the lack of synchronization between the tropical and the synodic years is due to the difference in the length of the lunar day and the solar day and it recommends that in order to synchronise the two years an extra month should be added at half *yuga* and another at the end of the *yuga* (*YJ. 37*). This is similar to the scheme of intercalation adopted by the *cāturmāsya-yājins* as described in *MS.I.10.8.* and that which follows from *TS.VII.4.8*. What is perhaps surprising is that the surviving recensions of *Vedāṅga Jyotiṣa* do not mention the correction that is necessary every eighth *yuga* to stop the runaway accumulation of the difference between the number of days in five tropical years and a *yuga* (see above). This correction is so glaringly necessary and obvious that it is inconceivable that the composers of *Vedāṅga Jyotiṣa* would have missed it. Is it possible that this vital passage was lost between Lagadha, the putative composer of *Vedāṅga Jyotiṣa*, and Śuci the redactor? We may never know. In spite of this lacuna the similarities between these crucial aspects of the calendar of *Vedāṅga Jyotiṣa* and the calendar in *Maitrāyaṇīya Saṃhitā* and that which can be derived from *Taittirīya Saṃhitā* are so striking that the conclusion that the origins of *Vedāṅga Jyotiṣa* are in the earlier *Saṃhitās* and the *Brāhmaṇas* is inescapable.

## 4. CONCLUSIONS

The five year intercalation scheme (*yuga*) is unique to South Asia. This scheme is first explicitly mentioned in *Vedāṅga Jyotiṣa* but its origins have baffled Ideologists for over hundred years. The concept of intercalation can be traced back to *Ṛgveda*, the earliest Vedic text available to us. A number of intercalation schemes can be inferred from post-*Ṛgvedic* *Saṃhitās* and *Brāhmaṇas*. These schemes would have synchronised the synodic or the ritual year and the tropical year over short periods (ten to twenty years) but failed over longer periods. An algorithm for intercalation is given in *Maitrāyaṇīya Saṃhitā* and this scheme of intercalation is based on an intercalation period of five (tropical) years although this period is not called a *yuga*. This *Saṃhitā* does not mention the correction that is necessary to the five year intercalation scheme to keep the synodic year synchronised with the tropical year over extended periods. A 'perfect' scheme of intercalation can be obtained from mathematical expression of passages in *Taittirīya*
512 INDIAN JOURNAL OF HISTORY OF SCIENCE
*Saṃhitā* that describe the start of the tropical year and the ritual year. This scheme has the five year intercalation period built into it and the correction that this scheme requires. This scheme also includes all the calendrical features that can be traced back to the earliest Vedic texts and are important to the Vedic culture, namely, six seasons of two months each, a canonical month of thirty days, a canonical (ritual) year of $360$ days, a thirteenth month and the number 'five' (through the five year period) which plays an important part in Vedic thinking. The scheme would keep the synodic year and the tropical year synchronised over hundreds of year.
Far from "no systematic intercalation scheme" the Āryas appear to have 'experimented' with a number of schemes. They may even have discovered a perfect scheme. A number of features of the calendar of *Vedāṅga Jyotiṣa* can be traced back to the *Saṃhitās* and the *Brāhmaṇas* and is possible that in *Vedāṅga Jyotiṣa* (whenever it was composed) the Āryas were only formalizing a calendar that had been in use for decades earlier.
